How your grade works

Your grade is built from exactly two ingredients:

  1. Standards measure what you learned — the fifteen specific skills, each marked Met or Not Yet.
  2. Engagement credits protect the process that produces learning — credit for showing up prepared and doing the work.

There are no points, no weighted averages, no partial credit, and no curve. There is also no penalty for struggling early — read on.

Standards: Met or Not Yet

Every standard gets multiple opportunities across the semester (checkpoints, in-class work, the project, the defense — the schedule shows where each appears). The marking rules:

  • Convergent standards (the ones with checkable right answers) require two successful demonstrations, on different occasions.
  • Divergent standards (open-ended) require one demonstration at “Meets” quality, with the right to revise and resubmit once per artifact.
  • “Not Yet” means not yet. A standard missed in week 4 and demonstrated in week 11 is Met. Your grade records where you ended up, because that’s what learning a skill means. (The one bound: reassessment happens at the scheduled reappearances of each standard, plus token-purchased retries — see Tokens. It is not unlimited-on-demand.)

Checkpoints

Four Tuesdays — weeks 4, 6, 8, and 10 — open with a checkpoint: about twenty minutes, individual, on paper, no AI, no notes. Each checkpoint assesses a few convergent standards and re-offers items from earlier checkpoints.

Checkpoints are also where a small amount of unaided writing lives: expect to produce a short function by hand from a given spec — five lines or so, no AI in the room. Why, in a course that delegates production? Because verification requires a minimum writing fluency: you cannot audit a language you can’t produce a sentence in. Checkpoint items are marked Met / Not Yet per standard like everything else; there is no checkpoint “score,” and a rough checkpoint costs you nothing that the re-offers can’t repair. (Rough checkpoints are also how Base Camp invitations happen — see Base Camp — which is a resource, not a consequence.)

Engagement credits: the process

This course runs on committed preparation and in-room collaboration — the opening votes, the table arguments, the critiques. Those can’t be graded on correctness without poisoning them (nobody argues honestly at a table where wrong answers cost points). So they’re credited for good-faith completion instead:

Earned by Credits Roughly available
Bring-to-class item, committed on paper, checked at the opening vote 1 each ~22
Thursday activity deliverable 2–3 each ~24
Pin-up participation & review quality (weeks 12–13) 3 each 6
Total available ~50

Rules of the road: credits are checked for committed good-faith work — a wrong answer with reasoning earns full credit; a blank or a transparently uncommitted scribble earns none. Credits are earned in the room (that’s the point), with tokens as the illness/life valve. And note the arithmetic below is deliberately forgiving: an A requires 40 of ~50, so you can fully miss about a fifth of everything and feel no effect.

Why credits at all? Because without them, the rational move is to skip the preparation and coast on the tables’ work — and this course is the tables. Credits make the preparation count without making it a quiz. (Design borrowed from Robert Talbert’s pairing of engagement credits with standards-based grading.)

Project-period credits are individual

The 8 credits of the project period (pin-ups #1 and #2, the mock defense) are awarded per person, informed by teammate evaluation — two short, description-based evaluations, one formative in week 13 and one summative in finals week. The default outcome is nothing: ordinary teams keep ordinary credits. The instrument exists for the teammate no one can describe. Full mechanics, including exactly what the form asks and why it asks for descriptions rather than ratings, on the peer evaluation page.

Tokens

Everyone starts the semester with 3 tokens. Spend one for: a 48-hour deadline extension; an excused activity (the engagement credits without attending — illness, interviews, life); or one extra reassessment of a convergent standard outside its scheduled windows. No questions asked, no documentation required — that’s what the tokens are for. Unspent tokens convert to 1 engagement credit each at semester’s end.

The grade table

Your letter grade is the highest row where you meet every requirement in that row:

Grade Standards Met (of 15) Must include Engagement credits
A ≥ 14 P5 ≥ 40
B ≥ 12 P5 ≥ 32
C ≥ 10 — ≥ 24
D ≥ 7 — ≥ 16

Plus: meet all of your row and at least one requirement of the row above → attach a plus (e.g., B+). Minus: exactly one requirement falls short of your row, and it lands in the row directly below → you get the higher grade with a minus rather than dropping a full letter. F is reserved for missing the D row.

Why P5 is necessary for the A and B: P5 — holding your whole project in your head — is what the defense measures, and it’s the standard that certifies all the others were yours. An A in a course about directing and auditing computation, held by someone who can’t explain their own project, would be a false statement. We don’t issue those.

Three worked examples

Student 1 — the straightforward case. Meets 14 standards including P5 (only S4 still Not Yet after the project), earns 43 engagement credits, spends one token on an extension during a brutal midterm week. Check the A row: 14 ≥ 14 ✓, P5 ✓, 43 ≥ 40 ✓. Grade: A. The two unspent tokens add 2 credits (45), which changes nothing.

Student 2 — the slow start (read this one if you’re nervous). Bombs the first checkpoint: P1 and P4 both Not Yet in week 4, and it feels catastrophic. It isn’t — both standards are re-offered at the week-6 and week-8 checkpoints, and Student 2 demonstrated each twice by mid-semester. Finishes with 12 standards Met including P5 (S2 and A3 fell short on the project report; his revision closed A3 but not S2, and F1 never came together), and 41 engagement credits, because Student 2 did the work all semester even while the checkpoints were rough. B row: 12 ≥ 12 ✓, P5 ✓, 41 ≥ 32 ✓ → at least a B. Plus check: meet ≥ 1 requirement of the A row? The 41 credits clear the A row’s 40 ✓. Grade: B+. Note the early disasters cost nothing. The grade records where we ended up.

Student 3 — the ghost (read this one if you’re planning to skip class). Brilliant, and knows it: 13 standards Met including P5, some on first attempt. But treats class as optional — skips the readings’ commitments, misses most Thursdays — and finishes with 22 engagement credits, having spent no tokens (which convert: 22 + 3 = 25). B row: standards ✓ ✓, but 25 < 32 ✗. C row: 13 ≥ 10 ✓, 25 ≥ 24 ✓ → at least a C. Plus check: meets two B-row requirements (standards, P5) → Grade: C+. Is that harsh for someone with 13 standards? Well, this course is also about the process — prepare, commit, argue, critique — because that’s the half of the job the standards can’t see. Just show up! Ten more Thursdays of just participating and we’re at a B+.
