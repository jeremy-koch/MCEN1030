How your grade works

Standards + engagement credits

Your grade is built from two ingredients, plus the final exam modifier:

  1. Standards measure what you learned. There are fourteen specific skills that you will be assessed on, each marked Met or Not Yet.
  2. Engagement credits are about the process that produces learning — credit for showing up prepared and doing the work.

Those two set a floor for your letter grade. The final exam — a written defense of your own project — then decides where inside that letter you land.

There are no points, no weighted averages, no partial credit, and no curve. Maybe most interesting: you can recover if you struggle early and still receive an A grade!

Standards: the concepts

Every standard gets multiple opportunities across the semester. The rules:

  • Convergent standards (the ones with checkable right answers) require two successful demonstrations on different occasions.
  • Divergent standards (open-ended) require one demonstration at “Meets” quality, with the right to revise and resubmit once per artifact.
  • “Not Yet” means you aren’t there yet. But if you are Not Yet on a standard in week 4, then successfully demonstrate it in week 11, the standard is Met and you will receive full credit. (Though don’t forget convergent standards require two successful demonstrations.)
  • Reassessment happens at the scheduled reappearances of each standard, plus token-purchased retries — see Tokens.

Checkpoints

Four Tuesday classes (weeks 4, 6, 8, and 10) begin with a checkpoint: about twenty minutes, individual, on paper, no AI, no notes. Each checkpoint assesses a few convergent standards and re-offers items from earlier checkpoints. At least one of a convergent standard’s two demonstrations must be in-class.

Expect to produce a short function by hand from a given spec — five lines or so, no AI. Why, in a course that delegates production to LLMs, are we having you write code yourself from scratch? Because verification requires an understanding of coding elements and at least a bit of fluency with the language.

Engagement credits: the process

Part of your grade will be based on before-class preparation and in-class collaboration. These won’t be graded on correctness, and instead are based on good-faith completion:

Earned by Credits Roughly available
Bring-to-class item, written on paper, checked at the start of class 1 each ~22
Thursday activity deliverable 2–3 each ~24
Pin-up participation & review quality (weeks 12–13) 3 each 6
Total available ~50

Rules: a wrong answer with detailed reasoning earns full credit; a blank or a transparently rushed scribble earns none. Credits are earned in class and can’t be replaced with out of class work (though you can safely miss a few, and tokens can be used to make up some credits).

Tokens

Everyone starts the semester with 3 tokens. Spend one for: a 48-hour deadline extension; an excused activity (the engagement credits without attending — illness, interviews, etc.); or one extra reassessment of a convergent standard outside its scheduled windows. No questions asked, no documentation required. Unspent tokens convert to 1 engagement credit each at semester’s end.

The grade table

Your grade floor is the highest row where you meet every requirement in that row:

Floor Standards Met (of 14) Engagement credits
A- ≥ 13 ≥ 40
B- ≥ 11 ≥ 32
C- ≥ 9 ≥ 24
D- ≥ 7 ≥ 16

Missing the D- row is an F.

Meeting these requirements earns you the minus grade, the final exam decides whether you stay there or move up.

The final exam

The final is a written defense of your own project, assessed as:

Assessment What happens
Weak You stay at your floor (e.g. B-)
Solid Up to the whole grade (e.g. up to a B)
Distinguished Up to the plus (e.g. up to a B+)

Note about the ceiling: the university doesn’t issue A+ grades, so at the A- row a solid final and a distinguished final both land on A.

Three worked examples

Student 1 — the straightforward case. Meets 13 standards, earns 43 engagement credits, spends one token on an extension during a tough midterm week. Check the A- row: 13 ≥ 13 ✓, 43 ≥ 40 ✓ → floor of A-. Then the final: we might ask to trace the path the data takes through the functions and how something is filtered out, and let’s say Student 1 answers that well → solid → Grade: A.

For Student 1, the two unspent tokens add 2 credits (45), but that doesn’t change anything about the grade.

Student 2 — the slow start (read this one if you’re nervous). Bombs the first checkpoint, messing up two standards. No big deal — both are re-offered at the week-6 and week-8 checkpoints, and after also succeeding at them in the homework, Student 2 demonstrates each twice by mid-semester. Finishes with 11 standards Met. Then 41 engagement credits, having done the work all semester even while the checkpoints were rough. B- row: 11 ≥ 11 ✓, 41 ≥ 32 ✓ → floor of B-. Note that those 41 credits clear the A- row’s bar, but 11 standards don’t — the floor is the highest row where every requirement is met. Then the defense: Student 2 knows everything about the project, teammates’ files included → distinguished → Grade: B+.

Student 3 — the ghost (read this one if you’re planning to skip class). Brilliant, and knows it: 12 standards Met, some on first attempt. But treats class as optional — skips the readings’ commitments, misses most Thursdays — and finishes with 22 engagement credits, having spent no tokens (which convert: 22 + 3 = 25). A- row: 12 < 13 ✗. B- row: 12 ≥ 11 ✓, but 25 < 32 ✗. C- row: 12 ≥ 9 ✓, 25 ≥ 24 ✓ → floor of C-. The defense is, predictably, excellent → distinguished → Grade: C+. Is that harsh for someone with 12 standards and a great defense? Well, this course is also about the process — prepare, commit, argue, critique. Ten more Thursdays of just participating and we would be at a B+.
