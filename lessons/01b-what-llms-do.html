What does an LLM actually do?

Week 1, Lesson B — read before Thursday

You will use AI consistently in this course and you might have some experience already. (If not, don’t worry!) Let’s spend one lesson on what’s it’s actually doing.

Think First

Recall

  1. Your phone’s keyboard suggests the next word as you type. How do you think it decides what to suggest?
  2. Have you ever confidently “remembered” something that turned out to be wrong?

Look Ahead

  1. If two students give an AI the exact same prompt, will they get the exact same code back?
  2. Why might an AI be better at writing code than at doing arithmetic?

The short version

A large language model (LLM) does one thing:

Given some text, predict a plausible next small chunk of text.

That’s it. Everything else — the code-writing, the explanations, the apparent reasoning — emerges from doing that one thing extremely well, at enormous scale.

The “small chunks” are called tokens — usually a word or piece of a word. thermal might be one token; thermodynamics might be three. (Different than the tokens involved in our standards-based grading scheme!)

The model learned to do this by, in effect, reading a colossal amount of text — books, articles, documentation, and importantly for us, billions of lines of code — and adjusting billions (perhaps trillions) of internal numerical knobs until its “next-chunk” predictions got very good. This is what we mean when we talk about a new LLM “model” — there are more parameters (knobs) and updated tunings of those knobs that do a better job of creating accurate/plausible bodies of text.

Three consequences

Everything strange about LLM behavior falls out of that mechanism.

1. Its answers are plausible, but might not be true

The model produces text that is statistically likely to follow your prompt — the text sounds like what the correct answer might sound like. It is not reasoning based on physics, it has read a bunch of text written by humans who reasoned based on physics. Then it realized, statistically, that whenever those humans mention “physics”, “black holes”, and “singularity”, it is probably appropriate to use the word “Hawking”, and a likely word after that is “radiation”.

One concerning thing about LLMs is that they are increasingly being trained on text generated by LLMs — so much internet content is generated by LLMs that the LLMs are reinforcing their own tendencies and possibly that will lead to quality degrading over generations.

Usually, the most plausible continuation of an engineering question is the correct answer, which is why these tools are often useful. But when the model doesn’t “know,” it does not stop and say so. It keeps generating plausible text — a medical treatment suggestion that doesn’t apply to the situation (dangerous!), a citation to a paper that doesn’t exist, a recipe with a cup of vanilla instead of a teaspoon. This is often called a hallucination, and the dangerous part is that the LLM can be just as confident about a hallucination as it is about real information.

Important

There is no tone difference between an LLM being right and an LLM being wrong. Verify!

2. Same prompt, different answers

Prediction produces a menu of plausible next chunks, each with a probability. By design, the model doesn’t always pick the single most likely one. A bit of controlled randomness in the choice makes output more varied and, usually, better. Run the same prompt twice and the choices fork somewhere, and once forked, the two continuations keep diverging.

LLMs with a higher “temperature” value (not the physical temperature) are more likely to choose a token with a lower probability. An LLM with zero temperature chooses the most probable token every time — for a given prompt, it creates the same output every time.

This is why, on Thursday, 15 tables prompting for the same task will produce 15 different programs — different variable names, different structures, sometimes different algorithms, occasionally different answers. Thinking about that is an important aspect of this class!

3. A good specification tames the LLM

A prompt is the text you supply to the LLM, maybe a question. An effective prompt is thoughtful… it’s not like a Google search where you just say some relevant words (“lyrics steely dan”) and then you, yourself, look through the results to find what you are looking for. You should be supplying instructions, relevant context, and constraints.

Does anyone else find it annoying that Google’s first result is now AI? Whenever I don’t know exactly what I am looking for, being able to scan the first few results is usually what it takes to figure it out; and whenever I do know what I am looking for, I am not interested in Gemini’s mediocre summary!

“Build me a diet plan” is too general to be immediately useful… are you trying to lose weight? Gain weight? Gain muscle? Add more fiber? Eliminate carbs? Allergic to nuts? The LLM will probably follow up with questions like that, and you’ll end up somewhere good, but sometimes it might make assumptions for you that you have to spend time undoing. (“Yuck, it wants me to eat fish three times a week!”)

Rather than arguing with the LLM for 15 minutes, wasting a bunch of water and electricity in the process, you should be thoughtful about your specifications.

The context window is the cap on how much of that text the model can “see” at once — your current prompt, along with (at least some of) your previous questions in the same conversation, any files you’ve included. And maybe you’ve given the LLM some baseline instructions that are automatically applied to all conversations. There is a limit on how much of that information can be included in a given text-generation step, and even if it were all included, the more context it has, the greater chance of getting confused! I suspect part of the model development that is happening is about doing a better job understanding what in the context window is most relevant to your current prompt.

Why is it so good at code, anyway?

The biggest reason: the training data included billions of lines of code, much of it from working programs, and a lot of solid documentation from the code creators themselves ( find_peaks). So there is a strong statistical signal that these are very relevant words for this task.

But furthermore: code is checkable. Compare

“You can’t fix every problem, so just focus on doing good work in your own corner of it.”

with

“It is not our part to master all the tides of the world, but to do what is in us for the succour of those years wherein we are set, uprooting the evil in the fields that we know, so that those who live after may have clean earth to till.”

How can you judge that the first one is “wrong”? Based on our understanding of how LLMs work, every new word/token in that line is a completely reasonable continuation of the sentence. The second one, by Tolkien, is better, but not “more correct”.

Meanwhile: if code doesn’t run, something is wrong, and we further will get an output from code that can possibly be confirmed/validated against known results. Modern LLMs are able to run code on the cloud to confirm they perform as intended, and they are increasingly willing to compare against test cases for you: if you need some code to find the area under a curve, an LLM might write it, run it, and compare against the known area under \(y=x\) from 0 to 1. Basically, the LLMs are doing their own small verification. That probably isn’t sufficient to confirm that the code does what it is supposed to, but it is necessary that the code works for at least that basic problem.

We will do our own audit and verification of the code: read through it to make sure it follows a logic we agree with, and test its output in several ways. We may need to iterate — the AI gives us code that seems like it is 90% of the way to succeeding at the job, but there is an error, and so we need to work with the AI to revise. Unlike prose, we can identify specifics about what is wrong and eventually arrive at something that is verified correct.

Meanwhile: LLMs are historically mediocre at arithmetic — predicting a text representation of numbers in a calculation is not the same as calculating.

The stance on LLMs in this course

No doubt, LLMs are tools that can augment your abilities. A few years ago I might have manually combined 40 spreadsheets into one (which maybe would have taken an hour, but was annoying), or I might have written code to do that for me (which probably would have taken four hours to figure out… should have just done it manually arggg). Now I can ask an LLM to give me the Python code to do it, and the project is done in 5 minutes.

I have become more ambitious and capable in some aspects of my work because I know I can get a head start using AI. But note: I am the one who came up with the idea, and I needed to describe the problem and some of the necessary steps. And, importantly, I am the one responsible if something breaks along the way, and so I need to have a strategy to confirm that everything made it into the finished project successfully.

I think the following is very much in the spirit of coding in 2026: Think of LLMs as your extremely fast, extremely well-read, overconfident junior collaborator. You can use them to quickly retrieve information, you can ask them to help you write code, they can help troubleshoot problems. But ultimately, you are setting the expectations (specifications), and you are responsible for the project (so you better do some verification). And, further, there will be times when you have to show that you, too, actually know how to do the stuff (the checkpoints).

One more term you’ll see a lot: an agent is capable of actually making changes on your computer — creating new folders, creating/editing files, maybe sending emails — rather than giving you text in a web browser that you have to move around. Still basically a junior collaborator: you still provide the details and check the results, you just have to do less manual labor.

Answer these:

  1. Two students give an LLM the identical prompt for a projectile program. Predict: (a) identical code, (b) different code, same numerical answers, (c) different code, possibly different answers. Write one sentence defending your pick.

  2. The LLM hands you a projectile program and it runs without any errors, and produces a nice plot. List every distinct reason you can think of that its output might still be wrong.
