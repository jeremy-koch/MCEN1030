Simulate I: stepping into the future

Week 9 — read before Tuesday

Week 1’s last promise: solving the “unsolvable.” Today you build a time machine out of parts you already own — and I mean that literally: this lesson contains no new Python. A function (week 3), a loop (week 5), a plot (week 6). Only the idea is new, and the idea is enormous.

Think First

Recall

  1. Week 5: when must you use a loop instead of array arithmetic? (Hint: when each pass depends on the one before.)
  2. Hot coffee on your desk: does it cool faster at the start or near the end? Why do you believe that?

Look Ahead

  1. If you know a system’s rate of change right now, how far into the future dare you predict?
  2. Two simulations of the same coffee disagree. How do you decide which — if either — to trust?

Two kinds of model

Everything so far evaluated algebraic relations: plug in \(h\), get fall time. Question in, answer out, done. But most engineering systems don’t hand you an answer formula — they hand you a rule for how things change: the coffee cools at a rate proportional to how much hotter it is than the room. Physics gives the rate; you want the future. The bridge between them is the oldest trick in computational science:

New value = old value + (rate of change) × (small time step)

That’s it. That’s the time machine. Apply the rule, step forward a little, re-evaluate the rate (it changed! the coffee is cooler now), step again. March into the future one small step at a time.

This is called Euler’s method, and it’s the ancestor of every simulation you’ve ever seen — weather forecasts, car-crash models, video-game physics. They use fancier steps; the marching idea is identical.

By hand first — three steps, on paper

Coffee at \(T = 90\) °C in a \(20\) °C room, cooling law \(\text{rate} = -k(T - 20)\) with \(k = 0.05\) per minute, step size \(\Delta t = 1\) minute.

Step 1: rate \(= -0.05(90-20) = -3.5\) °C/min, so \(T \leftarrow 90 + (-3.5)(1) = 86.5\). Step 2: rate \(= -0.05(86.5-20) = -3.325\), so \(T \leftarrow 86.5 - 3.325 = 83.175\). Step 3: you. (Commit before checking: rate \(= -0.05(63.175) = -3.159\), so \(T \approx 80.02\).)

Notice what your hands just learned: the rate itself shrinks as the coffee cools — fast cooling early, slow later, exactly matching your Think First intuition. The hand-march is the hand estimate (A1) for everything that follows.

The code — assembled entirely from prior weeks

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def cooling_rate(T, T_room=20, k=0.05):
    """Rate of temperature change (deg C per min), Newton's law of cooling."""
    return -k * (T - T_room)

dt = 1.0                      # step size, minutes
T = 90.0                      # initial condition
temps = [T]
for step in range(120):       # march 120 minutes into the future
    T = T + cooling_rate(T) * dt      # THE line: new = old + rate * step
    temps.append(T)

plt.plot(np.arange(121) * dt, temps)
plt.xlabel("Time (min)"); plt.ylabel("Coffee temperature (°C)")

Read the loop body and recognize old friends: it’s the accumulator pattern (week 5) where the thing accumulating is the future, and T = T + ... is week 2’s “gets, not equals” carrying the entire simulation on its back. This is the convergence moment of the course: nothing here is new except the power.

Step size, and the first way simulations lie

Run it again with dt = 25 (steps of 25 minutes). The simulation still runs, still plots, still looks confident — and it is now measurably wrong: each step charges 25 minutes in a straight line at a rate that was only true at the step’s start. Whether that straight-line charge lands you above or below the true curve is exactly your Bring-to-class question, so no spoilers here — but crank dt truly huge and the “coffee” can oscillate, or plunge below room temperature: a thermodynamic impossibility, rendered in a professional-looking plot.

Important

This is S3’s first failure mode — numerical error, wrongness you injected by discretizing, with no physics mistake anywhere. The standard check costs one rerun: halve the step and compare. If the answer barely moves, the step was fine. If it shifts, keep halving until it stops. A simulation whose answer depends on dt isn’t an answer yet.

Verify against a known answer — while you still can

This particular problem is one we can also solve exactly with calculus: \(T(t) = 20 + 70e^{-0.05t}\). Plot both — with dt = 1 they’re nearly indistinguishable; the marching machine works. Now the strategic point: we verified the method on a problem with a known answer because next week’s problems won’t have one. That’s the whole professional pattern — calibrate your tools where truth is available, then carry the earned trust (and the halve-the-step habit) into the wilderness where you actually need simulation. It’s also why this week’s toy problem isn’t a toy: it’s your instrument calibration.

On paper: (1) March the coffee model three steps by hand with \(\Delta t = 2\) minutes (same \(k\), same start — show the arithmetic). (2) Commit with one sentence of reasoning: the true cooling curve is concave-up (steep early, flattening); Euler steps travel in straight lines at the starting rate of each step — so does a too-big step land above or below the true curve? Thursday: build-a-model lab, where each table gets a different system to march (draining tank, falling object with drag, charging capacitor, heating apartment) and we discover every table’s model is wrong in its own special way. That’s not a bug in the plan.
