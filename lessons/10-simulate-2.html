Simulate II: assumptions, trust & what-ifs

Week 10 — read before Tuesday

Last week you built the machine. This week: judgment — what to put in it, when to believe it, and how to ask it questions. This is the most engineering week of the course; there is exactly one line of new code in it.

Think First

Recall

  1. Week 9’s coffee model: name two real physical effects it completely ignored. Did the answer seem to suffer?
  2. Week 1: “have intuition about the physics” was called an old skill with renewed importance. This is the week that cashes that check.

Look Ahead

  1. “All models are wrong; some are useful.” How can both halves be true at once?
  2. A model matched every test you ran. A colleague wants to use it 20% outside the range you tested. What do you say?

All models are wrong — on purpose

The coffee model ignored evaporation, the mug’s heat capacity, the room warming slightly, stirring. Deliberately. A model is not a copy of reality; it’s a selective simplification, and the selection is the engineering. Include everything and you have an intractable mess; include too little and you have tidy nonsense. The skill (standard S2) is threefold: know what you included, know what you excluded, and know under what circumstances an exclusion stops being ignorable. Ignoring air drag on a dropped wrench: fine. On a dropped feather: absurd. On a wrench dropped from an airplane: the exclusion quietly became the answer.

The assumptions audit

A concrete ritual for any model — yours or an AI’s. Three columns on paper: Included / Excluded / Excluded matters when… For projectile motion, the classic audit: constant gravity (excluded: variation with altitude — matters for rockets, not baseballs); no air drag (matters when speed is high, mass is low, or distances are long — i.e., for most real projectiles, which is why artillery tables existed); no spin (tell that to a golf ball); flat Earth (matters beyond a few km). Auditing takes five minutes, and it converts “the model said 47 meters” into “the model said 47 meters, and here’s the box that number lives inside” — which is the difference between a student answer and an engineering answer.

The three ways models fail — the full S3

Numerical — the discretization lies. Week 9’s too-big step: error you injected by chopping time into chunks. Diagnosis: halve the step, compare. You own this one already.

Physical — a missing effect bites. The model faithfully solves equations that no longer describe the situation: drag-free artillery, the coffee model applied to a mug sitting in sunlight. Diagnosis: the assumptions audit, plus comparison against even one real measurement.

Extrapolation — a good model, used outside its tested range. The most dangerous, because the model earned genuine trust inside its box and nothing visible changes when you step outside. The night before the Challenger launch, engineers had O-ring seal data from prior flights — the coldest around 12 °C. Launch morning was around 0 °C. The judgment call treated behavior beyond the data as if the trend politely continued. It did not. Seven people died, and “we had no data at that temperature” is now the textbook sentence every engineer is taught to say out loud, before the decision.

Important

The three failures have three different diagnostics — step-halving, assumptions audit, know-your-data-range — which is why S3 makes you name which kind of wrongness you suspect. “The model might be off” is a mood; “we’re extrapolating 12 degrees past our coldest data point” is an engineering statement.

What-if: the model as a question-answering machine

Now the payoff for all this discipline — the reason we build models at all. A trusted model is an experiment factory: what if the mug is thicker? The room colder? The tank valve half-open? Each what-if is a parameter sweep (week 5!) pointed at a simulation (week 9!):

for k in np.linspace(0.01, 0.10, 10):      # ten hypothetical mug/lid designs
    temps = simulate_cooling(k=k, dt=1.0)   # your week-9 machine, functionized
    plt.plot(time, temps, label=f"k={k:.2f}")

Ten futures, one plot, one second. The S4 skill isn’t running this — it’s interpreting it: which knob moves the output a lot (sensitive — measure it carefully, control it tightly) and which barely matters (insensitive — stop agonizing over it). Sensitivity is where simulations stop being homework and start being design.

Interrogating an AI-built model

The AI will happily build any model you ask for — on assumptions it chooses and does not volunteer. Ask for “a projectile simulation” and drag is silently in or silently out, coin-flip. So the A4 workflow for models adds one prompt, always: “State every assumption this model makes and its valid range.” The reply is genuinely useful — and then you audit it too, because the model’s actual assumptions live in the code, not in the model’s self-description. (The code is the territory; the AI’s summary is a map drawn by an enthusiastic intern.) Assumptions audit, on everything, no exceptions — including, in week 14, on your own project, where the examiner will be me.

For a damped oscillator model — a mass on a spring with friction, position marched forward exactly like the coffee — commit on paper: (1) two assumptions you’d bet it makes; (2) for each, one situation where that assumption breaks; (3) a prediction with one sentence of physics: if the damping coefficient doubles, what happens to the motion? Thursday: model critique. Each table receives another table’s week-9 model plus its assumption audit, and reviews it S2/S3-style — is the audit honest? which failure mode is nearest? — before we vote on the damping question and settle it by running the sweep live.
