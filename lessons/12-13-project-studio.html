Project studio

Weeks 12–13 — how studio time works

No new reading for two weeks. Class becomes studio: your team building a real computational project at the tables, instructors circulating, and structured critique events where the work goes public. This page is the operating manual.

The project

Your team (3–4, formed at launch) picks from the menu:

  • Audio forensics — load a recording, process the signal, extract something real: the frequency of a passing siren (and hence, Doppler willing, its speed), the tempo of a song, the echo delay of a room.
  • Video tracking — track an object through video frames (trackpy) and turn pixels into physics: a projectile’s drag, a pendulum’s damping, a teammate’s gait.
  • Live-data instrument — build on week 8: fetch from an API or phone sensor on a schedule, process the stream, produce an auto-updating analysis (ISS pass predictor, weather-trend monitor, stairwell-step counter).
  • Design-space explorer — a serious parameter sweep of a real system (heat sink, bike frame member, pump selection) culminating in a “here is the best design and why” recommendation.
  • Industry option — a problem provided by an external partner, scope negotiated at launch. (Menu subject to expansion; pitch me your own at launch if you have one — the bar is “genuinely multi-file, genuinely verifiable.”)

Deliverables, due finals week: (1) the code — a real multi-file program, every file annotated; (2) the spec history — your prompts and specifications as they evolved, kept as you go, not reconstructed; (3) verification evidence — hand checks, test cases, sanity arguments, honestly including the ones that failed; (4) a short published report — a Quarto page presenting the problem, method, results, and uncertainty (A2 and A3 on full display).

Desk crits (Tuesdays)

Instructors and TAs circulate table to table, ~10 focused minutes per team. The rule that makes desk crits work: bring your ugliest problem, not your best work. A desk crit spent showing off is a wasted desk crit; the ten minutes exist to unstick the thing that’s stuck. Have ready: what you’re trying to do, what’s happening instead, and what you’ve already tried (that last one is the difference between a consultation and a rescue).

Pin-ups (Thursdays)

Work goes up — screens turned outward or printouts on the wall — and tables review each other’s projects in rotation.

Pin-up #1 (week 12): specs and verification plans. Before major code exists, the thinking gets critiqued: is the specification complete? What did they assume (S2)? How will they know it works (the verification plan)? Catching a spec hole in week 12 costs a conversation; catching it in week 14 costs your weekend.

Pin-up #2 (week 13): working artifacts. Running code, real output, draft plots. Now the critique vocabulary is the standards page itself — the divergent standards are the review questions: What’s assumed (S2)? How was it verified (A1/A4)? Is the plot honest (A2)? Could I rerun this from what’s documented (A3)?

Critique etiquette, which is itself being practiced (engineers review each other’s work for a living, and doing it well is a career skill): critique the work, never the people; questions beat verdicts (“what happens if the input file is empty?” beats “this seems fragile”); every critique names one thing that’s working, specifically; and “cleanest” claims must be defended with criteria — matches the spec, verified, honest about uncertainty, rerunnable — not vibes.

The divergence rule

Teams that chose the same menu item will have produced different programs — different architectures, different assumptions, sometimes different answers from similar data. Pin-up #2 deliberately pairs same-project teams: first each explains the other team’s architecture back to them (a brutal and wonderful P4 exercise), then the argument: which choices were better, and by what criterion? This is the “everyone used AI and the results came out different — now understand why” engine, running at full scale, on work you care about.

Individual accountability — read this twice

Divide the labor. Do not divide the understanding. The defense (week 14 and finals) is individual, and it will ask you about parts of the project you didn’t personally produce — that’s not a gotcha, it’s the definition of being on an engineering team. The cheapest insurance is built into the deliverables: the annotation discipline (every file annotated in the team’s own words) plus a standing team habit worth stealing — end each studio session with five minutes of “walk me through what you did today.” A teammate who can’t explain a file to you in week 12 is a teammate who can’t explain it to me in finals, and you now share that problem. One more structural fact: project-period engagement credits are individual, informed by peer evaluation — description-based and default-harmless; read that page rather than the rumor of it.

Standards in play

Everything, but the center of gravity: S2, S4, A2, A3, A4 through the deliverables and pin-ups, and above all P5 — holding a whole project in your head — which these two weeks exist to build and the defense exists to measure.
