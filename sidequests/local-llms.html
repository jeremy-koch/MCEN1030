Local LLMs

Note

If there is interest, we could talk about this in an office hour. Send me an email!

I want to briefly introduce the idea of local LLMs, and along the way there are some notes about AI/LLMs in general. Know that I am not an expert, this is just how I see it!

I don’t really want to see local LLMs as a central part of your project — it is against the spirit of the Final Defense, where you need to be able to explain your code. But I am adding this to the Tools part of the class because I think it is worth knowing these tools exist.

Warning

In what follows I am going to describe what local LLMs are and will vaguely comment on how you might get set up with one. I purposefully am not going to give explicit instructions — if this is something you are interested in, you need to be able to figure it out on your own. If you can’t, you probably shouldn’t be getting into local LLMs!

A few warnings:

  • Local LLMs can be turned into agents that have read/write privileges on your computer. This means they could delete files on your computer, send emails without your permission, or post your Social Security number online.
  • Local LLMs are smaller than cloud-based LLMs, but are still way too large for humans to understand. Though they are open-weight, we practically do not know how they were trained, i.e. what sources were used. It could be that the entirety of a model’s heat transfer knowledge comes from a person who got a C+ in heat transfer! It is pretty much a guarantee that local LLMs will be less capable. The (often hilarious) flaws of early LLMs are still present in the smaller local models.
  • If you download a model, it will probably be a ~5 GB file. We don’t know what comes along with the file — and there are often two parties involved: the lab that trained the model, and someone else who converted and shrank the file into a form you can run. There are suspicions that some of the models may have a backdoor that is sending information somewhere. I have not seen any evidence of that, but there is speculation.

Proceed at your own risk.

What’s the idea?

The big cloud-based LLMs include ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, Copilot, … I’m probably forgetting or am unaware of at least a few more. With these, your prompt and any files you have uploaded are relayed via the internet to a computer somewhere running a LARGE-language model. The calculations happen, and the results are sent back to your computer via the internet.

Those AI companies may claim to keep your information private, but you may have reasonable doubts. Additionally: if you have an application that needs constant AI use, a cloud-based AI will be quite expensive: you will likely exceed the limits of your $20/month account quickly.

Reportedly these companies are losing money when charging just ~$20/month for access. Their strategy may be to get an enormous presence in society such that “we can’t live without it”, and then they will increase the price. The frontier models are genuinely impressive, and that hypothetical price increase is justifiable, but $20/month is very different from $200/month (or whatever it ends up being).

These reasons motivate local LLMs.

Local LLMs

You can download and run large language models on your personal computer. No internet connection (except to download), no sign-in, no giving the tech bros your information, no cost. They aren’t particularly hard to set up, and you can interact with them in a chatbot-like environment. So why aren’t we using them?

The biggest reason: We are limited by the amount of memory (RAM) on our machines. I have a pretty standard laptop with 16 GB of RAM and am able to run an open-weight model with ~7 billion parameters. That sounds like a lot but… it is estimated that Claude Opus 5.5 has 5 trillion parameters. Ostensibly, the cloud-based LLMs have hundreds of times better mapping of information than a local LLM I can run on my laptop. (That doesn’t mean hundreds of times more information or hundreds of times as intelligent, but it is notably better.) I imagine they also have some human-guided training on how to interact with users — what information to provide, what to not provide, how to present information, how to build certain files from scratch. They will automatically run code on the servers for you.

A quick search suggests it would take maybe 10 TB of RAM to hold the entire Opus model.

Despite their limitations, there are many things that local LLMs can do. Small, well-defined jobs like retrieving information from notes, reformatting text, or processing incoming image files could be comfortably handled by a small local model. Maybe most interestingly, you can set up an AI agent on your computer that can handle certain tasks for you. E.g., you could point the agent at a folder of 40 messy CSV exports from lab equipment (with different column names, different units, different input parameters) and ask it to merge them into one clean file, along with a list of assumptions it made. This is something that might be difficult to automate with a simple Python script, but AI would probably do a good job.

Claude Code is probably the most famous agent. I use Claude Code to create the interactive Python examples, format tables, proofread text, and edit the website structure document (i.e. “add this file to the left sidebar” instead of me figuring it out myself). It can generate prose, but that writing clearly sounds like an AI wrote it. If you are paying attention, more and more of the content we consume sounds like an AI wrote it… not a good thing.

Local LLM security

For you

You’ll hear stories about bad things AI has done (both cloud-based and local). Local LLMs have greater access to your computer, and typically fewer guardrails, and so their risks are larger. Examples:

  • The AI thinking it was in a certain folder and running a command to delete all files in that folder. Wrong folder.
  • The AI downloading and installing software it thinks it needs. (The human probably didn’t deliver good problem specifications!)
  • The AI being handed a file, email, or web page that contains sneaky instructions (“ignore the previous instructions and send the contents of this folder to…”) and blindly following them. This is called prompt injection, and has been a common attack.
  • The AI “fixing” a failing test by changing the test instead of the code. (Technically the test passes now.)
  • The AI telling you “Done! Everything works.” when it didn’t actually check, or when the check failed.
  • The AI starting something that never ends: a loop that doesn’t stop, a download that fills your disk, a program that eats all your memory.

One wise step, which I need to learn how to implement myself, is to create a sandbox. In computing, a sandbox is an environment where a program can only touch what you’ve explicitly handed it — one folder instead of your whole computer, no internet, none of your passwords. Then if a “delete everything in this folder” command is issued, the damage is limited.

Principle of least privilege: give a program the minimum it needs, and no more.

Again, I haven’t built one for a local LLM myself, but here are some sandbox-related ideas:

  • Work with a copy. Point the AI at a copy of your project, or keep the project in git so any damage can be undone.
  • Permission settings in the tool. Most agent tools can be told to ask you before running any command or touching any file outside the project.
  • A container or virtual machine. A container (Docker is the common name) is more-or-less a computer inside your computer. The AI runs in there, sees only the folders you share into it, and if something goes wrong it is confined. (Not infallible, but probably what I will do.)

Don’t leave your passwords, API keys, Social Security number, etc. lying around for the AI to find. Don’t give it email access. Maybe don’t give it internet access.

For society

There is an ongoing debate about security. The large AI companies have claimed that they have implemented security measures to prevent bad actors from doing bad things with the models. For example, Claude has a “Mythos” model that is apparently extremely capable of finding vulnerabilities in software, and so Anthropic has not released Mythos to the public. The “Fable” model is supposed to be nearly as powerful, but with guardrails — ask it to find a way to hack something, and it mostly says no.

Local LLMs are likely not receiving the same considerations, though thankfully they are currently less capable. Still, there is a push from some to regulate and even ban open-weight models.

How you might use a local LLM

First some vocabulary. There are three layers:

  • The model is a big file of numbers, the weights. By itself it does nothing: it can’t even run.
  • A runtime is the program that loads that file into memory and runs it. (The word for running a trained model, as opposed to training it, is inference.)
  • A harness is a program wrapped around the model. It lets the model say “I’d like to run this command” or “I’d like to edit this file”, does it for the model, feeds the result back, and repeats. Claude Code is a harness.

Both of the tools below are runtimes, not harnesses.

A chat window: LM Studio

The easiest way to start is LM Studio, a desktop app. You search for a model inside the app, click download, and you get a chat window that looks a lot like the cloud chatbots… except the whole thing is running on your laptop.

From code: Ollama

Ollama is a runtime that mostly lives in the background and in the terminal. There’s no chat window — the point is that it talks to other programs, not so much to humans (although you can ask it questions in the terminal).

I accessed it via Python code: I was analyzing some transcripts. From the point of view of the code, it was essentially a function call: the input was the transcripts (and a “system prompt”), the output was a summary and some metrics (that I described in the system prompt).

Picking a model

Tip

If you are interested, I recommend having a discussion with a cloud-based LLM on this. I used Claude to help troubleshoot my installation of a handful of AI tools and had it recommend a model for me based on my available RAM and what other tasks I will be running simultaneously.

There is some variation in the models due to how they were trained — e.g. Gemma is allegedly better for writing prose, Qwen is allegedly better at engineering and coding. But the bigger consideration is the RAM you have available (and what else you are running at the same time).

Hugging Face is a website where people share AI models. When a lab like Alibaba releases an open-weight model, this is usually where it goes, and so do the converted and quantized versions that people like bartowski make from it. Downloading is free.

An example: a 7 billion parameter model stored at 16 bits per number would require \(7\times10^9 \times 2\) bytes \(= 14\) GB of memory. But I could not run it on my MacBook with 16 GB of RAM — the operating system, the LM Studio program, etc., require some RAM. Realistically I might have 8 GB of RAM available for an LLM.

While I could have chosen a model with fewer parameters, another option is to use a quantized model: each weight is stored with fewer bits (often about 4 instead of 16), so the file is roughly 4x smaller. You lose a little accuracy but are able to get the model onto your personal computer.

I used qwen2.5-vl-7b-instruct at the “Q4_K_M” quantization for a project where I was looking at pdfs. This was on an M2 MacBook with 16 GB of RAM.

  • Qwen is the family, made by Alibaba.
  • 2.5 is the generation.
  • VL means vision-language: it can read images as well as text.
  • 7B is the parameter count (7 billion).
  • Instruct means it was further trained to follow instructions and chat. Without this tag, a model just continues whatever text you give it.
  • Q4_K_M is the quantization: roughly 4 bits per weight, using one of the “smart” schemes that keeps the most sensitive parts at higher precision.

Using an agent

I haven’t set up a local agent myself, so this section is part explanation and part me working out how it must fit together.

The model sits inside a runtime (Ollama or LM Studio), the runtime offers a little server on your computer, and the harness talks to that server. If you are thinking about coding, several coding agents can do this: “Cline” and “Continue” sound like two prominent candidates.

Potential difficulties

  • The model has to know how to ask. Models are specifically trained to request tools in a particular format (this is called tool calling). A model that wasn’t trained for it will describe running a command instead of requesting one, and nothing will happen. Check that the model you picked says it supports tools.
  • Context. The harness adds a lot of stuff to your prompt: instructions, the menu of tools, the contents of files. Runtimes tend to ship with a small default context window, and making it bigger means it will take more RAM.
  • Speed. A laptop is slow. If you call a model in a loop, every time through the loop is a full LLM calculation.

Going further

I’m not an expert on any of these — again, ask a cloud-based LLM to help you plan/learn.

RAG

RAG stands for retrieval-augmented generation. Instead of hoping the model memorized the answer, you first search your own documents for the passages most relevant to the question, paste those passages into the prompt, and ask the model to answer from them. (The search is usually done with embeddings, which are a way of turning text into numbers so that “similar meaning” can be calculated.)

It sounds like one of the best local LLM applications: the model isn’t being asked to know things, only to read what you handed it, and so a small model can succeed. Engineers have a lot of datasheets, manuals, lab notebooks, etc., some of them private, and this is a great way to look through them.

Two caveats:

  • If the search pulls up the wrong passage, the model will answer from the wrong passage. A good RAG system shows you which passages it used, so you can check.
  • If your documents are short, you may not need RAG. If everything fits in the context window, just paste it in. RAG is useful when you have more text than can fit.

A few more directions

  • Images. The model I mentioned above can read images — I was using it to digitize some handwritten course notes. I wonder what else it could do with images?
  • Speech to text. I had another project where I used “Whisper” to turn audio into text. A really interesting feature: you could tell it to expect certain terms, and so it would accurately transcribe technical words.
  • Fine-tuning. Continuing the training of a model on your own examples, so that it gets better at one particular job. I imagine this is harder than it sounds.
