Welcome

MCEN 1030 · Introduction to Engineering Computing

Welcome to MCEN 1030: Introduction to Engineering Computing, taught by Professor Jeremy Koch in the Fall 2026 semester.

When I took this course (in Fall 2006… oof), the big challenge for students was to figure out how to use fundamental programming ideas — loops, conditional statements, indexing — to achieve an engineering task. My professor gave very clean problem statements so that we could focus on writing the code, and we spent hours figuring out how to put the ideas in the right order, getting the syntax right, and debugging errors.

Today, we have AI (artificial intelligence), specifically LLMs (large language models). These have proven to be extremely capable of writing code based on a prompt (natural language instructions). Engineers need some understanding of how computation can be used in their projects, but they are not manually typing in for i in items: anymore… they are writing prompts that include something like “iterate through the items in that list” and having an LLM do the work.

We could argue about LLMs actually being a form of artificial “intelligence”… we’ll talk about how they are just statistics-based word-guessers.

What does that mean for an introduction to engineering computing class, if traditional problems have been conquered by AI? We still need to learn what is possible with computation…

  • Can we solve “unsolvable” differential equations like The Lorenz Equations?

how computer logic works…

  • a=5,b=a+5,a=10… what is the current value of b?

and how to interpret code…

  • what is happening with for i in items:?

but we will also learn how to integrate LLMs into our workflow. Importantly, we are not going to treat LLM-generated code as a “black box”1: we need to understand what is going on in our code. After all, if you end up using an LLM-generated result in your job and something explodes, you won’t be able to blame an LLM — it’s your fault!

Further, we’ll learn why LLMs are not 100% trustworthy and will see that they, too, often produce broken code. That doesn’t mean we give up on the project — if we understand how to talk about computational ideas, how to interpret results, and how to troubleshoot/debug, we can work with an LLM. The net effect will likely be that we produce better work, faster, as long as we humans are an active participant in the process.

The flip side of this coin is: if we don’t understand computational ideas and are just hoping the LLM figures out what to do based on a vague description of the problem, we are going to be in trouble.

The big idea

For decades, Production — thinking about the code elements, structure, and syntax, and doing the typing — was the bottleneck, and so intro courses like this one spent nearly all their time on it. That bottleneck is gone: AI tools produce working code (from a good description) in seconds. The new bottlenecks are the two highlighted boxes:

Specification means describing the problem precisely: the inputs and outputs, what has to happen in the middle, what to do at the edges. If you can’t articulate the problem, no tool — human or AI — can solve it for you.

Verification means refusing to trust a black box: sanity-checking results against hand estimates, testing edge cases, knowing the ways a program (or a model, or an AI) goes wrong.

You will still read and run and modify plenty of code in this course — you can’t verify what you can’t read. But you will be graded on whether you can think computationally: frame a problem, direct the tools, and audit the results. That is what engineers actually do!

How a week works

Before class, you’ll read one lesson on this site (~20 minutes). Most of the code on these pages executes right in your browser and is editable. Every lesson ends with a Bring to class box: one question you answer on paper before you arrive.

More serious coding will happen in Google Colab, MATLAB Online, or Python/MATLAB on your own machine — the getting going page has instructions.

Class time is mostly group work at the tables. Early in the semester, mostly short problems with right answers. Later it shifts toward open-ended problems with design thinking. The readings introduce the concepts, and in class you’ll actually implement them.

Get extra practice on Mondays (optional). Base Camp early (the beginner on-ramp, checkpoint practice, …), Side Quests later (build a tiny language model, 3D-print a parameter sweep, …). No grades attached.

Checkpoints happen on Tuesdays (weeks 4, 6, 8, 10): twenty-minute “mini-exams”, individual, on paper, with no AI. See the grading page.

Homework problems are mostly about real mechanical engineering and will be formatted to include four steps: specify, annotate, verify, report. The homework page explains the format.

AI policy

You are expected to use AI tools in this course. You are also completely responsible for everything they produce for you. Every assignment asks you to show your specification, annotate the generated code, and demonstrate your verification.

Grading

This course uses standards-based grading, based on:

  • a list of specific skills (the standards), each marked Met or Not Yet
  • and engagement credits for preparation and in-class work.

There is no partial credit, no point-scrounging, and no penalty for struggling early: you can demonstrate a standard later when it reappears, and your grade is where you end up, not where you started. See the grading page.
