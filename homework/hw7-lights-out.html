HW 7 · Lights out

Anchor week 10 · Standards: S1, S2, S3, S4, F1

Scenario

On Thursday we built a model of a house cooling after the power goes out, and we spent most of the period asking when to believe it. Now let’s point it at a real night.

On the afternoon of December 21, 2022, an arctic front came down the Front Range. Boulder went from about 6 °C at lunchtime to about −18 °C by 6 p.m., and it stayed brutally cold for two days. Suppose the power had gone out at 6 p.m. that evening. You’re writing a short memo for a landlord with a few houses in town: how long do the tenants have before the pipes are in danger, and what, if anything, would buy them more time?

This is the last homework of the semester, and it’s all about the Simulate theme: build the model (S1), decide what goes in it (S2), judge whether to trust it (S3), and use it to ask what-if questions (S4).

Due Wednesday, November 4, at 11:59 p.m.

This one is in MATLAB, the same as Thursday’s activity. The cheat sheet has the translations from Python. A Live Script (.mlx) exported to PDF is a good way to make the report, with the same four headings as before: Specify / Annotate / Verify / Report. AI use is welcome: say which parts it wrote in your spec. As in HW 6, we’re not grading the AI audit (A4) this time.

How to create the pdf (from an mlx file): Live Editor tab → Export → Export to PDF.

The model

From Thursday: each time step, the inside temperature moves toward the outside temperature by a fraction of the gap,

T_new = T - (T - T_out) / tau * dt

with tau (τ) the house’s time constant in hours. We’ll say τ = 30 hours for an ordinary house, and it’s somewhere between 15 (drafty) and 60 (very well insulated) in general. (We’ll go beyond as a “what if”.)

The new thing on this assignment: T_out isn’t constant anymore. It’s the real outside temperature, hour by hour, so each step uses whatever the outside temperature was during that hour.

The danger line. Plumbers usually tell people to keep the heat at 55 °F (about 13 °C) or higher in a cold snap, because pipes inside exterior walls run colder than the room. Let’s say 10 °C inside is when we start worrying and 4 °C is when pipes in the outside walls are probably freezing. (These are estimates.)

Data

The outside temperatures come from Open-Meteo, the same free weather service we used in week 8. Besides forecasts, it keeps a historical archive. The address is a base plus options after the ?:

https://archive-api.open-meteo.com/v1/archive?latitude=...&longitude=...&start_date=...&end_date=...&hourly=temperature_2m&timezone=America/Denver
option what it means what you want
latitude, longitude where Boulder: 40.015, −105.27
start_date, end_date the date range, as YYYY-MM-DD 2022-12-21 to 2022-12-24 (the end date is included, so this runs through 11 p.m. on Dec 24)
hourly which measurements temperature_2m (the air temperature 2 m above the ground, °C)
timezone which clock the times use America/Denver, so 6 p.m. means 6 p.m. in Boulder

In MATLAB, webread sends the request and turns the JSON reply into a struct, which is MATLAB’s version of the dictionary we used in week 8. Instead of info["hourly"], you write info.hourly:

url = '...';                  % full address, no spaces
info = webread(url);

Then look at what came back before using it (type info in the Command Window, then info.hourly, and keep digging). Where are the times? The temperatures? How many hours did you get? Which hour is 6 p.m. on December 21? (Remember: MATLAB counts from 1.)

Write down where it came from, in a comment or text block right after you fetch it: the full address, the date you fetched it, and the latitude and longitude Open-Meteo says it actually used. (Compare those with the ones you asked for. Interesting…)

If the request fails, try again in a minute. If Open-Meteo is down for a long stretch, there’s a saved copy on Canvas, but say in your spec that you used it.

Part 1 — Build it and check it

Write a function that takes the starting inside temperature, an array of hourly outside temperatures, τ, and the time step, and returns the times and inside temperatures. Use a time step smaller than an hour. Each hour’s outside temperature holds for every step inside that hour. (Is that a reasonable thing to assume? Put it in your audit in Part 3.)

Before trusting it, check it in two ways:

  1. Thursday’s case. Give it a constant −10 °C outside (an array where every hour is −10) and τ = 30. It should reach 10 °C at about 12 hours, as on Thursday.
  2. By hand. Using the real outside temperatures, compute the first three steps yourself and compare.

Part 2 — The real night

Start at 20 °C inside at 6 p.m. on December 21, with τ = 30. Run it through 11 p.m. on December 24, the last hour of data (78 hours in all).

  • When does the inside reach 10 °C? 4 °C? Give actual clock times.
  • Plot the inside and outside temperatures on one plot, with your danger lines drawn on it.
  • Look at the curve: does it make physical sense? Is the inside ever colder than the outside? If so, did the inside cool past the outside, or did the outside warm past the inside? Which of those should be possible?

Part 3 — What’s in the model, and what isn’t

Make an assumptions audit: a short list of what the model includes, then at least five things it leaves out. For each thing left out, finish the sentence “this would matter when/because…” in a line or two. Some places to look: what heats a house besides the furnace? Is one number really enough to describe “the house”? Is the temperature at the airport-ish grid point the temperature at the house? Is τ the same on a windy night as on a calm one? Is the danger line the right line?

Then pick one thing you left out and test whether leaving it out was OK. You get to choose how. Here’s a tool that handles several of them: any steady source of heat inside the house (people, a space heater, the oven) acts like making the outside a bit warmer. For an ordinary house:

heat source about how much warmer it acts
three people 1 °C
one space heater (1500 W, plugged into a generator or a big battery) 5 °C

so the model becomes T_new = T - (T - (T_out + extra)) / tau * dt. For something like sunlight, you’ll need your own estimate (Open-Meteo can also give you shortwave_radiation, the sunlight hitting the ground, in W/m², but that is ambitious!). Whatever you test: how much did it change the answer to the landlord’s question? Was it OK to leave out?

Part 4 — How far to trust it

Write a short paragraph about each of the three ways a model goes wrong, for this model, on this night:

  1. Numerical. Is your time step small enough? Show the evidence: halve it, and compare the clock times from Part 2.
  2. Physical. Which effect from your audit is most likely to make the answer wrong, and in which direction (too early or too late)?
  3. Extrapolation. τ = 30 is an estimate for an ordinary house on an ordinary night. How far outside “ordinary” is this night? What would you want to know before you’d promise anyone a number?

Part 5 — What if?

Now use the model to explore. Pose at least two what-if questions, run them, and say what you learned. One of them must be a sweep: run many values of one input and plot how the answer changes.

Some starting points:

  • Insulation. How does the time to 4 °C depend on τ? Sweep τ from 15 to 100 hours and plot the time to 4 °C against τ. Is it a straight line? How big does τ need to be for the house to stay above 4 °C for the first 24 hours?
  • The space heater. Does a space heater on a generator save the pipes? How much time does it buy?
  • Timing. What if the power had gone out at noon on December 21, before the front arrived? Or at 6 p.m. on December 23?
  • Your own. Anything the landlord might reasonably ask.

For each, interpret, don’t just report: which inputs move the answer a lot, and which barely matter? What would you tell someone choosing how to spend money on this?

Your submission

Step 1 — Specify & prompt.

  • The full data address, the date you fetched it, and the location Open-Meteo used.
  • Your function’s inputs and outputs, with units, and how it handles the hourly outside temperatures.
  • Your time step, and why.
  • Your what-if questions, and what you’ll vary in each.
  • If you still need F1, say so here, and we’ll assess your data fetching and provenance for it.

If AI wrote any of your code, include your prompt and the model name and date.

Step 2 — Annotate. Three lines (or short blocks), each quoted in backticks, with a sentence or two in your own words about what it does:

  1. the update line, inside the loop;
  2. the line that picks the outside temperature for the current step;
  3. the line that finds the time the house reaches 4 °C.

Step 3 — Verify. Your two checks from Part 1, and the time-step halving from Part 4.

Step 4 — Report. A one-page memo for the landlord:

  1. The answer, in a sentence: how long until the pipes are in danger, for an ordinary house, on that night.
  2. The figure: inside and outside temperature through the outage, with the danger lines.
  3. What would buy more time, from your what-ifs, with numbers.
  4. How sure: the effects you left out and whether they matter, and how far outside the model’s comfort zone this night is.

Then attach your Part 3 audit, Part 4 paragraphs, and Part 5 plots after the memo.
