HW 5 · Where the wind blows

Anchor week 7 · Standards: P3, F2, A2, A4

Scenario

Your firm holds the survey rights over a 100 km × 100 km region of the plains and will build a 10 km × 10 km square wind farm somewhere inside it. Someone has already spent six months measuring the wind at every square kilometer of the region, which works out to 60,000 numbers. Your job is to turn those 60,000 numbers into one recommendation: where should the farm go?

See Submitting Homework for how to set up the report. Same look as the last three.

Remember the week 3 wind turbine? Power scales with \(v^3\). A site that’s only 5% windier makes about 16% more power, so small differences in wind speed are worth paying attention to.

Data

wind_survey.csv has four columns:

  • x_km, y_km: the site’s position, 0 to 99 km east and north from the region’s southwest corner. Each site is one square kilometer.
  • month: 1 through 6 (January through June).
  • wind_ms: that site’s average wind speed for that month, at turbine height, in m/s.

So there are 100 × 100 = 10,000 sites, each measured over 6 months: 60,000 rows. Download it and upload it to Colab’s Files panel, the way we did in week 6.

Tip

Right-click wind_survey.csv and choose “Download linked file” or similar.

A few new tools

The pandas lesson covered filtering and grouping on one column. This problem needs a few more tricks.

Important

I am not expecting you to memorize any of these, I haven’t. But you should know what they do when you see them! And, in this assignment, you’ll be annotating them.

Filtering on two conditions. We need to use &, and each condition needs its own parentheses:

Grouping on two columns, then making a map. Grouping by both coordinates gives one average per site, which squashes the months together. Then .unstack() spreads those averages out into a grid, with one row per y_km and one column per x_km:

With the real data, grid is 100 × 100. plt.imshow(grid, origin="lower") draws it as a heatmap, and plt.colorbar() adds the color scale. The origin="lower" part matters: without it, imshow puts row 0 at the top, which flips north and south on your map.

Slicing a square out of the grid. grid.to_numpy() turns the grid into a 2-D NumPy array, which can be sliced in both directions at once: [rows, columns], so [y, x].

Note the order: G[y, x], not G[x, y]. (Python won’t warn you if you flip them!)

Part 1 — Look first

Load the file, use head() and describe(). Do the minimum and maximum wind speeds make physical sense? (For calibration: 3 m/s is a light breeze, and 12 m/s will blow your hat off.)

Then make a map. Average each site over its six months (one number per square kilometer), arrange those 10,000 averages into a 100 × 100 table with groupby and unstack (see the tools above), and draw it as a heatmap: colorbar with units, axes labeled in km. Before you compute anything else, look at it and write down (in a text cell) where you would put the farm, and why. Add your prediction to the notebook… we will compare to what we calculate (and won’t penalize an incorrect guess).

Finally, find the single windiest site: which square kilometer has the highest six-month average, and what is it?

Part 2 — From wind to power

Back in week 3, a turbine made power \(P = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A v^3 C_p\). The wind power density is

\[ \text{power density} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho\,v^3 \qquad \text{(in W/m}^2\text{)} \]

the power carried through each square meter facing the wind. Use \(\rho = 1.0\) kg/m³, the same Denver air as week 3.

Add it as a new column, the same way the pandas lesson made temp_F out of temp_C: one line that computes it for all 60,000 rows at once. Then describe() the new column. For calibration: a typical wind here is about 8 m/s, and \(\tfrac{1}{2}(1.0)(8^3) = 256\) W/m². Are your numbers in that neighborhood?

Part 3 — The search

A 10 × 10 km square is set by its southwest corner \((x_0, y_0)\): it covers x_km from \(x_0\) to \(x_0 + 9\) and y_km from \(y_0\) to \(y_0 + 9\). We want the corner whose square has the highest average power density.

Make the same kind of 100 × 100 table you made in Part 1, but from the power column: one average power density per site. Then use the same pattern as the siren sweep: a nested loop, one loop over \(x_0\) and one over \(y_0\). For each corner, slice the square out of the grid, take its mean, and keep track of the best score so far along with the corner that produced it. Think carefully about the loop range: the square can’t hang off the edge of the region, so the biggest allowed \(x_0\) isn’t 99. How many candidate squares are there in total?

Then find the runner-up: the best square that doesn’t overlap the winner at all. (A square shifted over by 1 km is basically the same site.)

Compare the two:

  • Their average power density, and the margin as a percentage. Is it a few percent or a lot?
  • Their average wind speed.
  • Does the winner sit where you pointed in Part 1? Does it contain the windiest single site?

Part 4 — Month by month

A turbine company cares about the average, but it also cares about the bad months. Use the two-condition filter (four conditions, really: two on x_km, two on y_km) to pull out all the rows inside your winning square, then group by month and average the power density. Do the same for the runner-up.

How many rows should each square have? Check that you got that many.

Then put both squares’ monthly averages on one plot. Does the winner beat the runner-up in every month? Is one site steadier than the other? Does any of this change your recommendation? There isn’t a single right answer to that last question.

Now make that plot a second time, with one change: the y-axis. Draw one version with the axis starting at zero and one zoomed in to just the range of the data (plt.ylim sets it). Which one would you show your boss, and why? And which one would a competitor, trying to sell the runner-up site, want to show? (Think back to the plot clinic.)

Your submission

Step 1 — Specify & prompt.

  • Your Part 1 eyeball pick, written before any searching.
  • How you got/will get the power density column: the formula, the value of \(\rho\), and the units.
  • How a square is defined (by which corner), the loop ranges for \(x_0\) and \(y_0\), and how many squares that is.
  • How you’ll define “doesn’t overlap” for the runner-up.
  • What goes on the heatmap and the month-by-month plot.

Then your actual LLM prompt, with the model name and date.

Step 2 — Annotate. Four annotations this time. For each one, quote the line (or short block) in backticks, then write one or two sentences in your own words: what it does, and which spec decision it is related to. The four are:

  1. the line that makes the power density column. Check the exponent and the units;
  2. the line that groups by site and builds the grid (groupby and unstack);
  3. the line that slices one 10 × 10 square out of the grid. Which index is \(y\) and which is \(x\)?
  4. the line where the best-so-far gets updated.

If the AI made a decision you didn’t specify (a loop range, a value of \(\rho\), a default), point it out too.

Step 3 — Verify. Three checks:

  1. One site by hand. Pick any site, filter out its 6 rows, and print them. With a calculator, find the power density for each month and average them. Show the arithmetic in a text cell, and confirm it matches the power grid’s value for that site. Also compute \(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\,(\text{average wind})^3\) for the same site: is it bigger or smaller, and does that match the 8-and-12 example? Then check the grid’s orientation: does grid.loc[y, x] give the same number, or the number for the mirrored site?
  2. The square is where you think it is. Draw your winning square on the heatmap (plt.Rectangle will do it). Does it sit on top of the windy region the numbers say it does? If it lands somewhere calm, an \(x\) and a \(y\) got swapped somewhere.
  3. Counts. The number of candidate squares from Step 1, and the number of rows in your Part 4 square. Do both match what you predicted?

Step 4 — Report. A one-page site recommendation for the boss:

  1. The winning square (corner coordinates), its average power density and wind speed, the runner-up, and the margin.
  2. One honest map with both squares drawn on.
  3. The month-by-month plot (the version you chose, and one sentence on why) and a discussion of the consistency: does it change the optimum site?
  4. One or two sentences on the eye-versus-arithmetic question. Did your Part 1 pick win? If not, why not?
