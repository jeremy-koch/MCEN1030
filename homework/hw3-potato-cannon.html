HW 3 · The potato cannon

Anchor week 5 · Standards: P1, P2, S4, A4

Scenario

Your engineering club has a potato cannon. It launches a 5 cm × 8 cm cylinder of potato at about 75 m/s, and it sits on a pivot so you can set the tube’s angle above the ground. The club has put flags out on a field at 50 m, 75 m, 100 m, and so on out to 300 m, and wants to land a potato next to each one.

You don’t want to waste time with trial-and-error, so you’ll make a launch card: a small table taped to the PVC tube that says, for each distance, what angle to set. You’ll prepare a report with the card.

This is a very old job for computers. ENIAC, one of the first electronic computers, was built in the 1940s to compute cannon trajectory tables for the US Army. A person with a desk calculator needed roughly a day for a single trajectory. Your sweep will do almost two hundred of them in about a second.

See Submitting Homework for how to set up the report. Same look as HW 2.

The model

You can start with Thursday’s Round 4. The potato starts on the ground at speed \(v_0\) and angle \(\theta\) above horizontal:

\[ v_x = v_0 \cos\theta, \qquad v_y = v_0 \sin\theta \qquad \text{(at the start)} \]

and at every time step,

\[ \begin{align*} x_{\text{new}} &= x + v_x\,\Delta t & y_{\text{new}} &= y + v_y\,\Delta t \\ v_{x,\text{new}} &= v_x + a_x\,\Delta t & v_{y,\text{new}} &= v_y + a_y\,\Delta t \end{align*} \]

with drag pushing backwards along the path:

\[ a_x = -\frac{k}{m}\,v_x\,|V|, \qquad a_y = -g - \frac{k}{m}\,v_y\,|V|, \qquad |V| = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2} \]

For our potato, \(k/m = 0.00289\) (per meter), and \(g = 9.81\) m/s².

The model ignores wind, the potato’s spin and tumble, and the fact that no two potatoes are the same shape.

You may have written the stepper, or most of it, already. This assignment isn’t about the stepper. It’s about everything you have to build around the simulation.

Part 1 — One launch, as a function

Write landing_distance(angle_deg, ...), which returns how far away the potato lands, in meters. Speed, \(k/m\), and time step should all be inputs with sensible defaults. Write a docstring.

Two problems will come up right away. Neither has a single right answer, and both belong in your specification (see Step 1 below):

  • The stepper never lands exactly on the ground. On one step the potato is above it, the next step it’s below. So which \(x\) is “where it landed”?
  • You have to pick n_steps. What if the potato is still in the air when the loop runs out? The function has to do something… decide what.

Then check it in isolation, before you use it for anything (P1). With drag turned off, the range has a formula:

\[ R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g} \]

Check your function against it at 45° (show the arithmetic in your cell), then at 30° and 60°, which should tie. Then turn drag back on, run it to get a preliminary number, and run it again with half the time step. Does the landing distance change? By how much? Your time step dt is small enough when it stops having a noticeable effect on the result.

Part 2 — Sweep the angle

Run landing_distance for every whole-degree angle from 1° to 89°, with drag and without. Plot both range-vs-angle curves on one set of axes and find the best angle for each.

Then the sanity check:

Important

Without drag, the best angle should be 45°, and 30° and 60° should tie. Does your no-drag curve do that? If it doesn’t, stop here and find the problem.

With drag, the best angle is different. Compare your 30° and 60° launches. Without drag they tie. With drag, one of them is better. Speculate why the better one is better.

Part 3 — Make the card

The club’s flags are at 50, 75, 100, … 300 m, every 25 m. Your code has to find the angle for each one.

You already have what you need: your sweep is a list of angles paired with where they land. Reading it in the other direction (“I want to land here, so which angle?”) turns your range-vs-angle data into a launch card. Try plotting it that way: distance across the bottom, angle up the side.

You should not just squint at that plot or scroll through your sweep output to find the angles, and then write them down with a pencil. Build the card the same way you built the sweep, in two pieces:

  • One function that sets up one launch. Write launch_angle(distance, ...). It takes the distance to a flag and returns the angle to set. It’ll probably use your sweep’s angles and ranges as inputs. This is the same move as landing_distance, just run backward: that one answers “where does this angle land?”, and this one answers “what angle lands here?”
  • One loop that fills in the card. Make an array of slots with np.zeros(len(distances)), loop over the distances, call launch_angle for each, and drop each answer into its slot, just like the diameter sweep in For loops & indexing. Then print the card as a tidy table, one row per flag, with f-strings the way we printed the falling ball’s first few steps in A loop that remembers. The format code sets the decimal places ({angle:.1f} gives one, {angle:.0f} gives none).

When you’re done, test it for real: change the launch speed to 80 m/s in one place, rerun, and watch a new card come out. If that takes more than one edit, you’re not quite there. Change it back to 75 m/s for your report.

Tip

A place to start for launch_angle: np.argmin(np.abs(ranges - distance)) gives the index of the sweep point whose range is closest to distance. But look at your plot from Part 2 first. Most distances show up twice on that curve, and that one line doesn’t know it. Your function has to pick one.

Part 4 — How much should you trust the card?

Potato cannons aren’t precise. The launch speed changes from launch to launch, maybe by ±5 m/s, depending on how tightly the potato fits and how much pressure was built up. The angle adjustment is probably a bit imprecise too… ±1° is probably beyond the precision of an engineering student pointing some PVC pipe in a dirt field.

Pick the angle that gets you to the 200 m flag. Launch at your card’s angle at 70 and 80 m/s: where does it actually land? Then go back to 75 m/s and adjust the angle by ±1°: where does it actually land? Which matters more, the speed error or the angle error?

Your submission

Step 1 — Specify & prompt. This is the heart of this assignment. The stepper is eight lines you (mostly) already wrote. The specification is everything around it:

  • landing_distance: inputs, units, defaults, and what it returns. What counts as a valid angle? What does it do at 0°? At 90°? At −10°?
  • What “where it landed” means, given that the stepper jumps past the ground.
  • How many steps you simulate, how you picked that number, and what the function does if the potato hasn’t landed yet.
  • Your time step.
  • Your sweep spacing. Why whole degrees (or why not)?
  • launch_angle: inputs, units, and what it returns. When a distance can be reached at two angles, which one does it return, and why? What does it return for a flag the cannon can’t reach?
  • What the card looks like: its columns, its units, and how many decimal places.

Then your actual LLM prompt, with the model name and date.

Step 2 — Annotate. For each decision in your spec, point to the line that carries it out. Find the drag lines and confirm that \(|V|\) is the total speed, not a separate speed for each direction. If the AI made a decision you never specified (a default, a cutoff, which of the two angles it returns), talk about it.

Step 3 — Verify. The Part 1 checks: the range formula at 45° (with arithmetic), the 30°/60° tie, and the halved-dt comparison. The no-drag 45° from your sweep. Then spot-check the card: pick one row, run that angle through landing_distance, and show it lands where the card says.

Step 4 — Report. The launch card, as a table and as a plot. The best angle with drag, and your best explanation of why it isn’t 45°. Some discussion of how precise your card should be, referencing your results from Part 4. (That is, if your angle is accurate to ±1°, and your velocity is accurate to ±5 m/s, what is the ± on your distance?)
