HW 6 · Is Boulder warming?

Anchor week 9 · Standards: F1, P3, A2, A3

Scenario

Is Boulder warming, and by how much? We need a number we can put in a report and defend in a public meeting.

Fortunately, someone has been writing down Boulder’s temperature every day since 1893. You’re going to go get all of it (about 46,000 days) and turn it into that number. Along the way you’ll find that “how much has it warmed?” depends on choices you make.

See Submitting Homework for how to set up the report. Same look as before.

AI use is still welcome: say which parts it wrote in your spec. This time we’re not grading the AI audit (standard A4).

Data

NOAA (the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration) keeps the records for thousands of weather stations, and anyone can download them from a web address, no account needed, the same way we asked the ISS where it was in week 8. The address is a base plus a list of options after the ?:

https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/services/data/v1?dataset=daily-summaries&stations=...&startDate=...&endDate=...&dataTypes=...&units=...&format=csv

To be clear: you are going to need to edit this address.

The options you need to fill in:

option what it means what you want
stations which weather station USC00050848 (Boulder)
startDate, endDate the date range, as YYYY-MM-DD the whole record: 1893 through the end of 2025
dataTypes which measurements, separated by commas TMAX (each day’s high) and TMIN (each day’s low)
units metric or standard metric (°C)

Download it once. We’ll be running our code over and over while we work on it, and there’s no reason to ask NOAA for 46,000 rows every single time. So we’ll download the file once and work from our own copy:

  1. Put your full address together (no spaces anywhere), paste it into your browser’s address bar, and press Enter. NOAA answers with a file, and your browser downloads it. It takes a few seconds. (On an iPad, Safari puts it in the Files app, under Downloads.)

  2. Look at the file’s name: something like daily-summaries-2026-10-23T02-37-41.csv. That’s the date and time NOAA made it for you (in UTC, which is 6 hours ahead of Boulder until daylight saving time ends on November 1, and 7 hours after). Copy that name into your notebook, then rename the file to something friendlier, like boulder_daily.csv.

  3. Upload it to Colab’s Files panel, the way we did in week 6, and read it:

    import pandas as pd
df = pd.read_csv("boulder_daily.csv")

    Colab’s files vanish when the session ends, so you’ll upload it again each time you come back. That’s fine: it’s on your computer now, not NOAA’s.

pd.read_csv can also read straight from a web address: pd.read_csv(url). That works, but it downloads the whole record every time the cell runs. The server might get mad at you if you download it 40 times in an hour as you debug.

Remember: you can spread your code across several cells. It’s a good idea to put your imports and the line that loads the data in your first code cell, and the rest of your code in cells after it. Run the first cell once; the others you can rerun as often as you like while you work on them. (If Colab restarts, run the first cell again.)

Write down where it came from. In a text cell right after you load it: the full address you used, and the file name NOAA gave you (with its date and time).

Part 1 — Get the data and look at it

Download the data and load your copy. Then use head(), shape, and describe().

On a July afternoon in Boulder, a typical high is around 30 °C (86 °F). On a January night, a typical low is around −8 °C (18 °F). Do your numbers look like that? If they don’t, figure out why before going further. (Check the address you used.)

Then look at the extremes. Find the hottest high, the coldest high, the warmest low, and the coldest low in the whole record, each with its date. Are they believable? (For calibration: Boulder’s all-time records are around 40 °C and −36 °C.) If you don’t believe one, look at the days on either side of it. What do you think happened, and what will you do about it? Removing it, keeping it, and fixing it are all defensible, as long as you say which and why.

Finally, check on the missing data. Add a year column and count how many days each year actually has a TMAX measurement. Which years are badly incomplete? Pick one and find which months are missing.

Part 2 — From 46,000 days to a handful of numbers

Compute each year’s average high (TMAX) and average low (TMIN), along with the count of days used to calculate each average.

An incomplete year is a problem: a year that’s missing its winter months will look warm, and that has nothing to do with climate. Decide on a rule for which years to keep (for example, “at least N days of data”) and apply it. Your rule is part of your answer: write it down, and say how many years it keeps.

Then average your kept years by decade, for both the highs and the lows, into a small table. How many years went into each decade’s average?

Part 3 — The plot

Make one plot that tells the whole story: each year’s average high and average low, with the decade averages drawn on top so the reader can see the long-term pattern through the year-to-year noise. Labeled axes, units, legend.

Highs and lows are about 15 °C apart, so on one plot each line will look nearly flat. Is that a problem? Think about it the way we did in the plot clinic and on Checkpoint 3: what does the reader need to see, and what range on the axis would show it honestly? Two panels, one for highs and one for lows, is a reasonable solution.

Then, two more versions for comparison:

  1. The plot someone would make to convince the meeting that Boulder is heating up alarmingly.
  2. The plot someone would make to convince them that nothing is happening.

Use only real data, no fake numbers. (Your tools: the y-axis range, the start year, which variable you show, what you leave out.)

Part 4 — The number, and how sure you are

Climate scientists usually compare 30-year averages, long enough that one hot summer or one cold winter can’t swing the answer much. So:

  1. The headline. Average the yearly highs over 1996–2025. Average them over 1900–1929. How much warmer is the recent period? Do the same for the lows.
  2. The noise. How much do yearly average highs typically bounce around from one year to the next? (.std(), the standard deviation, from HW 4, is one good measure; the hottest year minus the coldest year is another.) Is your warming bigger or smaller than that bounce? What does that mean for someone who says “but last year was cool”?
  3. The baseline. Now use 1930–1959 as the early period instead. How much does the answer change? Look at your decade table: what was going on in the 1930s and 1950s? (Hint: the Dust Bowl.)
  4. The surprise. Compare the highs and the lows. Are they telling the same story? I can think of a few explanations for what you’ll find. Some ideas to start from: weather stations sometimes move, cities grow up around them, and the time of day someone reads the thermometer has changed over the years. Which explanation seems most likely to you, and what would you want to know to decide?

Optional, if you’d like a slope. np.polyfit(years, temps, 1) fits a straight line and returns its slope (in °C per year) and intercept. Multiply the slope by 10 for °C per decade. Try it starting from 1900, then 1930, 1950, 1980, and 2000. How much does the “warming rate” depend on where you start?

Your submission

Step 1 — Specify & prompt.

  • The full data address, and the file name NOAA gave you (it has the date and time in it).
  • What you did with each unbelievable value from Part 1, and why.
  • Your rule for keeping a year, and how many years survived it.
  • Which 30-year periods you compared, and why those.
  • What goes on the main plot.

If AI wrote any of your code, include your prompt, with the model name and date.

Step 2 — Annotate. Three lines (or short blocks), each quoted in backticks, with a sentence or two in your own words about what it does and which decision from Step 1 it carries out:

  1. the line that makes the yearly averages and counts;
  2. the line that applies your keep-a-year rule;
  3. the line that computes one of the 30-year averages.

Step 3 — Verify. Three checks:

  1. One year by hand. Pick a year. Filter its rows out of the daily data, and compute its average high and count directly. Do they match that year’s row in your yearly table?
  2. Units. Compute the average high for July of 2025 alone. Is it near the calibration number from Part 1?
  3. How much did that bad value matter? For one value you removed or fixed in Part 1, compute its year’s average both with and without it. How big is the difference, compared to the warming you found? (Was it worth fixing? Would it have been worth fixing if it had been −999?)

Step 4 — Report. A one-page memo for our report:

  1. The answer, in a sentence they can quote: how much warmer, measured how, compared with when.
  2. The figure from Part 3, the honest one.
  3. How sure: the year-to-year noise, how much the answer moves with the baseline, and what the lows say.
  4. Provenance: where the data came from, when, and every choice you made that could change the number (the bad values, the keep-a-year rule, the periods). Someone should be able to reproduce your number from this paragraph alone.
