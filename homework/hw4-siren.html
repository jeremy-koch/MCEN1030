HW 4 · The siren

Anchor week 6 · Tentative standards: P2, A3, A4

Scenario

A microphone was sitting in a field, set back some distance from a straight road, while an ambulance drove past. You know the sound: the pitch is high on the way in and drops as the ambulance goes by. Someone has already done the hard work and turned the audio recording into a file of pitch (frequency) against time. Your job is to answer one question: how fast was the ambulance going? And there are some other things we can learn from the data.

See Submitting Homework for how to set up the report. Same look as HW 2 and HW 3.

Data

siren_frequency.csv, with columns time_s and frequency_Hz. Download it and upload it to Colab’s Files panel, the way we did in week 6. (Colab forgets uploaded files between sessions so you’ll probably have to upload it multiple times.) Like real data, it’s noisy.

Tip

Right-click siren_frequency.csv and choose “Download linked file” or similar.

The model

A siren that’s coming toward you sounds higher than it really is, and one that’s moving away sounds lower. If a siren moves at speed \(U\) along a straight road, emits a true frequency \(f_0\), and passes closest to the microphone at time \(t_\text{pass}\), at a distance \(y_0\), then the microphone hears an “observed frequency”

\[ f_\text{obs}(t) = \frac{f_0}{1 - \dfrac{U}{c}\cos\theta(t)}, \qquad \cos(\theta(t)) = \frac{-U\,(t - t_\text{pass})}{\sqrt{U^2 (t-t_\text{pass})^2 + y_0^2}} \]

where \(c = 343\) m/s is the speed of sound. Let’s confirm this equation matches our intuition:

  • Long before the pass, \(t - t_\text{pass}\) is a large negative number, so \(\cos\theta \approx +1\), the denominator is a bit less than 1, and \(f_\text{obs}\) is a bit more than \(f_0\). Approaching, higher pitch. ✓
  • Long after the pass, \(\cos\theta \approx -1\) and the pitch is lower. ✓
  • Right at \(t_\text{pass}\), \(\cos\theta = 0\) and we hear exactly \(f_0\).

Count the unknowns: \(U\), \(f_0\), \(y_0\), \(t_\text{pass}\)… four things we don’t know, but we’ll figure them out from the data.

Part 1 — Look first

Load the file and plot it: markers, not lines, since these are measurements; labels with units. Before any fitting, just look at it and write down what you see.

Two of the four unknowns can be read nearly straight off this plot. Which two, and roughly what are their values? (Hint: what can we learn from the middle?) Reading them by eye is fine. Write both readings in a text cell, with units. You’ll use them in Part 4.

Part 2 — The model as a function

Write siren_freq(t, U, f0, y0, t_pass), which returns the predicted frequency at each time in the array t. Write a docstring with units.

Check it in isolation before you trust it. Use these made-up values, not the siren data: \(U = 20\) m/s, \(f_0 = 500\) Hz, \(y_0 = 10\) m, \(t_\text{pass} = 3\) s. Then do three checks, each in its own code cell:

  1. Call the function at t = 3 and confirm it returns 500.
  2. Call it at t = -1000 and t = 1000, stand-ins for “long before” and “long after.” Work out \(f_0 / (1 - U/c)\) and \(f_0 / (1 + U/c)\) with a calculator, show the arithmetic in a text cell, and compare.
  3. Plot it for t = np.linspace(0, 6, 200) and confirm the curve goes down as time goes on, not up.

Part 3 — An estimate with no fitting at all

Before we build anything clever, let’s get a number we can check against.

Far from the pass, the curve levels off into two flat stretches: a high one on the way in and a low one on the way out. Write code to do some averaging: take the mean of the first 20 frequencies and call it \(f_+\). Take the mean of the last 20 and call it \(f_-\). That’s one line each, using slicing ([:20] and [-20:]) and .mean() from week 4. Print both.

Now go back to the two limits in the model, \(f_+ = f_0/(1 - U/c)\) and \(f_- = f_0/(1 + U/c)\). That’s two equations with two unknowns (\(U\) and \(f_0\)). Solve them for \(U\) and \(f_0\) using algebra, not code. Write the steps in a text cell, then plug in your \(f_+\) and \(f_-\) with one line of Python each. Write down both estimates.

Part 4 — The sweep as a search

We will write a basic “fitting” process to determine the “best fit to the data”. The idea is to guess \(U\), \(f_0\), \(y_0\), and \(t_\text{pass}\), and then see how well the resulting equation replicates the data. Then guess another set of values and check, then guess another set and check… . It won’t be random guessing though — we will be systematic, sweeping through a reasonable range for the values. The tricky thing is: we need a way to score the guesses so that we can identify the best one.

Let’s do the following:

  • Decide on a way to keep score. For one set of guesses, run siren_freq at every time in the file and compare it with the measured frequencies. Boil the mismatch down to one number, where smaller means better. How you calculate that number is up to you (it’s a spec decision).

    Caution

    Simply adding up the differences doesn’t work — positive and negative differences cancel each other out.

  • Make the problem smaller. If we did a sweep over all four unknowns, with 40 possible values each, it would be 2.5 million guesses. That is not that crazy, but let’s simplify: we’ll hold \(t_\text{pass}\) and \(f_0\) fixed. Use your Part 1 estimate for \(t_\text{pass}\). For \(f_0\), use either your Part 1 estimate or the value your Part 3 algebra gives, and say which in your spec. Now you only have to sweep \(U\) and \(y_0\).

  • Create a “nested loop”. We can put a for loop inside a for loop:

    Let’s do that: loop over \(U\), and loop over \(y_0\) inside that loop. Every \((U,y_0)\) pair needs a score. As you go, keep track of the best score so far and the \(U\) and \(y_0\) that produced it.

Choosing the search ranges is part of your spec. Part 3 tells you roughly where \(U\) is, so search around it. For \(y_0\), the plot won’t tell you directly. The microphone was “set back some distance” from the road, which could mean 1 m or 100 m. You can always start with something coarse and run a finer sweep around the best spot afterward.

Write the sweep results to a file, siren_sweep.csv: three columns (\(U\), \(y_0\), score) with units in the header, and one row per guess. A 40 × 40 sweep, for example, is a 1,600-row file (plus header).

Tip

The nested loop makes this slightly awkward, because i alone no longer tells you which row you’re on. One way: pre-allocate three arrays of length (number of \(U\) values) × (number of \(y_0\) values). Then initialize a counter k = 0 before the loops, fill slot k of each array, and do k = k + 1 as the very last step in the inner loop.

Then np.savetxt the three arrays side by side. Peek at the first few lines only, e.g. print(open("siren_sweep.csv").read()[:200]). Printing thousands of rows will turn into dozens of pages of numbers in your PDF.

Part 5 — How sure are you?

Plot the data and your best-fit curve together: data as markers, model as a line. Then answer these three questions:

  1. How good is the best fit? Compare two numbers, both in Hz:
    • The noise. Run .std() on the first 20 frequencies. (The standard deviation: roughly, how far a typical point sits from the average of its neighbors. New function, same idea as .mean().)
    • The fit’s typical miss. At your best \(U\) and \(y_0\), compute the differences between model and data, square them, take the mean, then the square root. (If that’s what your score already was, you’re done. If you picked a different score, compute this one anyway so the two numbers are comparable.)
    Are they about the same size, or is the miss clearly bigger? If it’s clearly bigger, something in your model is a little off. Which of the two values you held fixed is the likeliest culprit, and why? (Answering that is required. Actually re-sweeping to fix it is optional.)
  2. Which parameter is the fit least sure about? Make two plots. First, hold \(U\) at its best value and plot the score against \(y_0\). Second, hold \(y_0\) at its best value and plot the score against \(U\). You can pull these from your sweep file, or recompute along each line with a finer step (which gives a smoother plot). On each, find how far you can move away from the best value before the score gets 10% worse than the best score. Write that as a percentage of the best value, e.g. “±4%.” The parameter with the bigger percentage is the one that is less certain.
  3. Based on our data and the fit to the observed frequency equation, what do we know about how the ambulance was moving? What things do we not know — i.e., what aspects of the motion are not captured in the data?

Your submission

Step 1 — Specify & prompt. Your spec covers the decisions, not the formula:

  • siren_freq: inputs, units, what it returns. Does it take one time or a whole array?
  • Which two unknowns you read off the plot, how you read them, and what values you got. Which \(f_0\) you’re holding fixed in Part 4 (Part 1 reading or Part 3 value).
  • Your score: which single number, and why that one?
  • Your search: the range and step size for \(U\) and for \(y_0\), and why. How many guesses is that in total?
  • What gets written to siren_sweep.csv, and what the header says.
  • The final plot: what’s on it, and how data and model are shown differently.

Then your actual LLM prompt, with the model name and date.

Step 2 — Annotate. At least six annotations. For each one, quote the line (or short block) in backticks, then write one or two sentences in your own words: what it does, and which spec decision it carries out. Five of them must be:

  1. the \(\cos\theta\) line. Check its sign against the “approaching means higher” argument above. A flipped sign produces a curve that goes the wrong way and still looks like a perfectly reasonable curve.
  2. the line that computes the score;
  3. the lines that set the search ranges for \(U\) and \(y_0\);
  4. the line where the best-so-far gets updated. What condition triggers it?
  5. the line that writes siren_sweep.csv.

The sixth is your choice. If the AI made a decision you didn’t specify (a search range, a score, a fixed value, a default), that’s a good choice. You can do more than six.

Step 3 — Verify. Three things:

  1. The three Part 2 checks, with the arithmetic shown.
  2. Your Part 3 algebra, your estimate of \(U\), and your sweep’s best \(U\) side by side. Do they agree to within a few percent? If not, which one do you believe, and why?
  3. The mph check: your best \(U\) in mph, and one sentence on whether that’s believable for an ambulance.

Step 4 — Report. Five things:

  1. The data-plus-fit plot.
  2. A small table of your best values for all four unknowns, with units. Mark which ones came from the sweep and which you held fixed.
  3. One sentence per value on how many digits you actually believe, using your Part 5 evidence.
  4. Your answers to the three Part 5 questions, with the numbers and plots that back them up.
  5. On the question of what we know and don’t know from the data, include at least two things for each.
