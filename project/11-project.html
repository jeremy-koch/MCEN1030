The project

For the last few weeks of the semester, your team will work on a computational project of your choosing. I want your topic choice to be open, though you should consider the notes that follow.

  • Pick something you are passionate about — you will do better work if so. You can put a link to your project repository on your resume… maybe it will help you get an internship. (We’ll do a Side Quest on creating GitHub repositories.)
  • Make sure you do something you understand. You are going to have to explain your project in the final exam. (And if you are asked about it in an interview, you don’t want to say “I don’t know, my partner/ChatGPT did that part”.) Every team member is responsible for knowing the entire project.
  • We have some time here, but not a lot of time. Getting the scope right is your first challenge. It may be tempting to be wildly ambitious because LLMs are allowed, but don’t forget: you have to defend it in the final!
  • The project is your last chance at the divergent standards (F1, S2, S4, A3, A4), so it should be shaped around the ones you still need. If you still need to demonstrate F1, think about a project that includes a fetch step; but if you are good on all the “fetch” standards, you don’t need to include a fetch step. Let’s talk about it if you are uncertain.

What are these “Pin-ups”?

Pin-up #1, Nov 12: Specs and plans. Before any major code exists, you will present your thinking to other teams, and the teams will give you feedback. Is the specification complete? What assumptions are being made? How is “success” going to be defined? Any recommendations?

Pin-up #2, Nov 19: Working code, real outputs, draft plots. Again, you will present your work to other teams, and the teams will give feedback. The project is due Wed Dec 2 and so you will have some time to act on the feedback.

Important

Critique the work, not the people. Ask questions without being overly judgmental (“What happens if the input file is empty?” is better than “This isn’t going to work.”)

Teams

I would like to see teams of three, with two or four possible with my approval.

Warning

The final defense will be completed as an individual, but every team member is responsible for knowing the entire project. The larger the team, the greater the chance that there is an aspect of the project you don’t know.

There will also be a Peer Review. The baseline is ~16 Engagement Credits, but if your Peer Review indicates that you have not contributed to the same level as your teammates, your Engagement Credits will be reduced, potentially to zero.

The project is due on Wed Dec 2, 11:59 p.m., the week after Fall Break. Decide now if you are going to work on Fall Break — you don’t have to, I am not encouraging you to. You will be presenting a basically-finished product before Fall Break anyway. But I don’t want half your team to be disappointed that the other half is unwilling/unable to put the finishing touches on the project over break. Set expectations this week, and if your team is not in agreement, consider not being a team.

Scope: can you explain it?

An AI will write you a 2,000-line dashboard by this afternoon, if you ask. The scope of the project is set by how much you can understand.

By Thursday you will define your big-picture core goal, and additionally at least one stretch goal. The core is the smallest version of the project that can be called a “success” — you collect and analyze the data, or build a dashboard, or find the optimum. The stretch is something that you are less certain is possible — an extra interactive feature, a model with a difficult parameter, etc.

I should also say: it is possible to make the scope too small. The project should be considerably bigger than a homework, should have multiple files, and should be worth having three people work on for three weeks. You’ll submit a proposal on Thursday and I’ll give some feedback.

Language/format

You may use Python or MATLAB for the project. Either way, it is going to look different than your homework submissions.

Namely: you can use a notebook (.ipynb, .mlx) to demonstrate your results, but your code should be a collection of basic Python/MATLAB files (.py, .m). And for some, a notebook file might not make sense — no problem, you can write the report in a word processor.

Tip

My recommendation is to install VS Code and work in Python. VS Code can be linked to a GitHub repository very easily — we’ll teach this in Monday’s “Side Quest” office hour. I encourage you to think about publishing this publicly and adding a link to your resume.

The power of GitHub is “version control”

What gets turned in

Due Wednesday, Dec 2, at 11:59 p.m.: details to follow.

  1. The code, as your file map: each file annotated by the person who led it.
  2. The spec history: your specs and prompts as they changed, kept as you go (not reconstructed in December).
  3. The report: the question, what you did, the answer, an honest plot, and how sure you are. Plus two sections that are easy to forget:
    • Provenance: where every piece of data came from and when, so someone else could rerun it.
    • What we cut, and why. Every good project drops something. Telling us what you dropped and why is engineering judgment, and it counts as evidence for S2 (deciding what to include and what to ignore). Cutting scope isn’t failing; cutting it silently is.
  4. Your own sections. For each file you led: the verification you did (hand checks, comparisons, the ones that failed), and the AI audit (spec, what the AI produced, your critique, what you changed). Your name goes on these. This is where your standards get assessed.
