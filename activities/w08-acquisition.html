Data acquisition lab

Week 8 Thursday · 2 engagement credits

Standards seeded: F1, F2, A3 · You’ll need: a phone with phyphox installed (free, iPhone and Android).

On Tuesday we fetched data that somebody else measured, today we make our own data. Your phone has an accelerometer inside it (it’s how the screen knows to rotate), and phyphox lets us record it.

Every group gets a slip with a group number, a secret task to record, and two other groups to investigate. You record your task and put the data where everyone can see it, but without saying what it was. Then you play detective on the other two groups’ data: from the squiggles alone, what did they do?

The instrument

Open phyphox and choose Acceleration (with g). Before you press play, put the phone flat on the table and make a prediction with your group: what will it read? The phone isn’t moving, so…?

Now press play and look.

It reads about 9.8 m/s², not zero. Ostensibly: inside the phone is a tiny weight on a tiny spring. Sitting on the table, the spring is holding the weight up against gravity, and the phone measures how hard the spring is working.

phyphox shows four things: acceleration along the phone’s x, y, and z directions (sideways, along its length, and out of the screen), and the absolute acceleration, the magnitude of the acceleration vector. Tilt the phone around while it’s recording and watch the x, y, z traces trade places while the absolute stays put.

The suspects

Every group’s secret task is one of these eight. Two groups have each task. Don’t tell anyone which one is yours!

  • Walk 10 steps
  • Run 10 steps
  • Jump once
  • Squat down and back up (feet stay on the ground)
  • Ride the elevator up (at least two floors)
  • Ride the elevator down (at least two floors)
  • Sit down in a chair
  • Stand up from a chair

Record

To make everyone’s data comparable, we’ll all record the same way. Firmly hold the phone flat against your chest with the screen facing out. The one exception is the elevator: put the phone flat on the elevator floor instead, because an elevator’s accelerations are gentle and your hand would drown them out.

Then:

  1. Press play, and hold still for about 3 seconds.
  2. Do your task.
  3. Hold still for another 3 seconds, then stop.

The still parts at the beginning and end are bookends. They tell anyone reading your data where “nothing happening” is, so the task stands out.

Record two or three times, look at the plots in phyphox, and keep the cleanest one. (If you have an elevator task, everyone in your group can ride together; one phone is enough.)

Share it

Our class has a shared Google Sheet (link on Canvas) with one tab per group, named G01 through G16.

Getting the numbers from your phone into the Sheet:

  1. In phyphox, tap the menu (the three dots) and choose Export data. Get it to your computer somehow… emailing it might be a good option.
  2. On your computer, find that file in Drive and open it with Google Sheets.
  3. Select everything with data in it (including the header row on top), copy it, and paste it into your group’s tab, starting at cell A1.

Please be careful to paste only into your own tab.

Loading a group’s data

Each tab of the Sheet has a web address we can read directly with pandas, just like Tuesday’s APIs. The data lives at a URL, and we go fetch it:

import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

sheet = "https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/SHEET_ID/gviz/tq?tqx=out:csv&sheet="

df = pd.read_csv(sheet + "G07")    # change G07 to whichever group you want
print(df.columns)

The part after the ? tells Google what we want: CSV format, and which tab. To look at a different group, change only the tab name at the end (G07 to whatever).

As always, print the columns before you use them. phyphox’s column names are long and include the units, something like "Absolute acceleration (m/s^2)".

t = df["Time (s)"]
a = df["Absolute acceleration (m/s^2)"]

plt.plot(t, a)
plt.xlabel("time (s)")
plt.ylabel("absolute acceleration (m/s²)")
plt.show()

Then plot the x, y, and z columns together on one plot (with a legend!) to see which way the phone was pointing and whether that changed.

Your own data first

Before investigating anybody else, get to know your own recording. Plot the absolute acceleration, then the three axes.

  • Find the bookends. What does “holding still” read?
  • Find your task. Where does it start and stop? Does its shape make sense, given what you remember doing?
  • Is anything surprising? (Bigger than you expected, smaller, a spike you can’t explain?)

Your own data is the only one where you know the answer, so this is your chance to learn what a trace looks like when you’re sure what happened.

Detective work

Now for the data sets you are to investigate. For each:

  1. Plot it. Absolute acceleration first, then the axes if you need more clues.
  2. Discuss. What do you expect each of the tasks to look like when viewed as accelerometer data? A measurement above 9.8 or below? How many “events”?
  3. Make a verdict, with evidence. Which task was it, and what in the plot convinced you? Use numbers from the plot, not just shapes: “10 peaks over about 3 seconds” not “it’s bumpy.”

We’ll release the answers after class on this webpage.

One last question

Code to plot your data (and the plot), then a text cell describing what you see; code to plot the first group you are investigating (and the plot), then a text cell describing your prediction and why; code to plot the second group you are investigating (and the plot), then a text cell describing your prediction and why.
