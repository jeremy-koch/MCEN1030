Which design wins?

Week 4 Thursday · 2 engagement credits

Standards seeded: P4, P1, A1 · You’ll need: Colab

Setting up

Same collection of wind data we’ve been working with, including a sensor glitch and an out-of-range gust. Then masked down to the hours the model is valid for.

Note the one change from the reading: turbine_power renamed an input variable to v_best, the wind speed the turbine’s blades are tuned for. This variable is related to the turbine design — we’re going to “sweep” through values for it.

Round 1 — warm-up (about 10 minutes)

Reproduce the reading’s diameter sweep, saving the answers. Loop over [80.0, 90.0, 100.0, 110.0, 120.0], append each day’s energy to a list, convert to an array, and report the winner with diameters[totals.argmax()].

Then answer two questions at your table before you go on:

  1. The 120 m rotor beats the 80 m rotor by a factor of 2.25. Where does 2.25 come from? (Hint: look at the equations.)
  2. You should see that the largest diameter wins. If we extended to a diameter of 130.0, would that be even better? 140.0 even better? Why, and what is the model missing/what does the problem statement not include?

Round 2 — the sweep (about 25 minutes)

Set the diameter at 100 m. Now sweep the design speed v_best from 6 to 16 m/s in steps of 1, and find the value that maximizes the day’s energy.

Before you run it, add a prediction to your notebook. The day’s average wind is 8.19 m/s. What is the best design_speed value? (Don’t worry if you guess wrong, leave your guess unchanged! It was a guess!)

If you are using Colab, this cell is mostly the same as the previous one… you can ignore the repeated parts, but the new cell in your notebook should include design_speeds = ... and then the loop you write. np.arange is a better version of Python’s built-in range… print that line to confirm what it does.

Run the sweep and report three things in a Colab cell:

  1. The best design speed, extracted with argmax and an index. (Don’t just go through the printout.)
  2. How much energy the best design makes versus the worst one. (Do this in the program, not by hand.)
  3. Whether your prediction was right. (It probably wasn’t!)
Important

The winner is not the average wind speed. Talk with your table for a few minutes to explain why. Hint: look at the equations.

Round 3 — break it (about 15 minutes)

Everyone at your table pick one of the following, making sure at least one person does each one. Spend a few minutes on it, then talk about what happened with your table.

(a) Resolution. You swept in steps of 1 m/s. Redo it in steps of 0.1. Does the answer move? Now do it in steps of 5. Does the sweep still find the maximum, or does it step straight over it? Write one sentence on how you’d choose a step size if you didn’t already know the answer.

(b) The mask. Rerun the whole sweep on the unmasked speeds array — glitch, gust, and all. Report the best design speed and the day’s energy. Both numbers are garbage; say precisely which bad reading did which damage, and how you’d have caught it if you didn’t already know the data was dirty.

(c) Parallel arrays. Deliberately break the thread: mask good but build your design_speeds array to a different length, or drop one entry from totals after the loop. Find out what Python does. Does it error? Sometimes yes, sometimes no — and “sometimes no” is the whole reason we’re talking about this.

Deliverables

Deliverable (2 EC): your Round 2 code, your written prediction (it’s fine if it’s wrong!), the best design speed with the index arithmetic that produced it, and a short paragraph explaining the gap between your prediction and the answer. Some discussion of Round 3, i.e. what you learned. (Include your code for Round 3, even if broken, but you don’t need to include all three.)

We see that the best value for design_speeds is not the average wind speed — this is because v_best is describing the most efficient situation for the turbine, not the most energy collected by the turbine. It is more efficient to get 40% of 1000, but 10% of 1,000,000 is more energy. (Numbers made up for dramatic effect!)

Round 3

Briefly:

(a) Resolution. Choosing a smaller step size improves the resolution of the calculation, at the cost of taking a longer time to complete (often not a big deal — these calculations finish in seconds, maybe, not hours). Choosing a larger step size might mean you step over the correct value. (There are also concerns regarding stability that you will learn about in Computational Methods!)

(b) The mask. Our energy equation has us cubing the velocity… cubing -999.0 gives us a very large negative number, but C_p also gives a negative number. So we land with something positive — no negative sign to warn us something is suspicious! We could catch this by doing an initial inspection on the data: speeds.max(), speeds.min() would give us the complete range of the data.

(c) Parallel arrays. When plotting two arrays together, if the arrays are different lengths, you will get an error. But, e.g., looking at diameters[totals.argmax()], where for whatever reason totals is a different length than diameters… that’s trouble!
