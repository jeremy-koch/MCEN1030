Adding drag

Week 5 Thursday · 2 engagement credits

Standards seeded: S1, S2, S3, A1 · You’ll need: Colab, Tuesday’s stepper

Tuesday we built a for loop to do some time stepping, applying it to a situation that we could solve theoretically. So… what was the point? Today we’re going to add some additional terms that make it so that there is no theoretical solution. But those additional terms make the calculations more accurate… so, as engineers, we want to include them!

Setting up

For anything bigger than a raindrop, the force of drag is proportional to the square of the speed. It is indeed a force, so it manifests through the acceleration term (\(F=ma\), right?):

\[ a = -g - \frac{k}{m}\,v\,|v| \]

The \(v\,|v|\) looks strange, but it’s just \(v^2\) with the sign of \(v\) still present — drag always pushes against you, so when you’re falling (negative \(v\)) that term comes out positive.

The parameter \(k\) bundles together the air density, the object’s cross-sectional area, and how streamlined it is. We’ll go ahead and assume some values for three different items:

object \(m\) (kg) \(k\)
balloon (25 cm) 0.003 0.01227
steel ball (2.5 cm) 0.0642 0.000123
phone, falling flat 0.19 0.0063
Tip

Below we’ll use a single variable called k_over_m. Let Python do the math for you: k_over_m=0.01227/0.003. Or even better: k_over_m=k_balloon/m_balloon.

Round 2 — same code, two different answers (about 25 minutes)

Run your code with all three objects from the table, plus the no-drag case, all from 10 m. Get the four fall times and four impact speeds somewhere you can compare them side by side (e.g., create a text cell after your code and record the results, maybe even as a Markdown table).

Three things to work out with your colleagues:

  1. The balloon takes about 4.6× as long as the no-drag formula predicts. The steel ball takes 0.4% longer. Same code, same height, same air. What is it about the two objects that produces such wildly different verdicts? (Look at \(k/m\), and say what it means physically.)

  2. Plot v against t for the balloon. Describe the shape in words — then talk about why it happens. Look at the acceleration formula and ask what value of \(v\) makes \(a = 0\).

  3. Compare the balloon’s impact speed to the value of \(v\) you just found. Same number? Should they be?

Important

One of my favorite quotes: “All models are wrong, but some are useful.” (From the statistician George Box.)

Models, by definition, are approximations of reality. We hopefully capture the relevant effects to make a good prediction, but we leave some details out — e.g., is it helpful to know the temperature of the object when determining its fall time? Where the surface imperfections are? Precisely how many nitrogen molecules are disturbed, and their speeds before and after? Probably not… even though they might affect the result in some (tiny) way.

Ignoring air resistance is perfectly good engineering for the steel ball — being right to within 0.4% and avoiding the nonlinear term is an excellent trade. But, applied to the balloon, our prediction is wrong by a factor of four and a half.

So “is it okay to ignore air resistance?” has no general answer. “Is it okay to ignore air resistance for this object, over this distance, at the precision I need?” has an answer — and you can compute it instead of guessing!

Round 3 — break it (about 15 minutes)

On Tuesday a smaller dt gave a better answer. Run the balloon from 10 m at dt = 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 0.5. For each, report both the landing time and the impact speed.

Then, on paper:

  1. At which dt does the answer stop being merely inaccurate and start being nonsense? How did you decide?
  2. One of those runs reports a landing time that looks more believable than the correct answer, while its impact speed is absurd. Which one?
  3. Plot y against t for the worst case. Describe, physically, what your balloon does. Would you have caught that from the numbers alone?
Caution

You may see nan appear — “not a number.” It means the arithmetic got to the poin that the computer cannot represent it anymore. That is not a Python bug, but does mean something went wrong with the calculation!

Round 4 — the potato leaves the building (about 20 minutes)

Everything so far has fallen straight down. Tuesday’s Challenge threw a ball forward and up. Let’s do that again, with drag, and with something more satisfying than a ball: a potato out of a potato cannon. A potato cannon is a tube with a potato jammed in one end and a burst of hot pressurized gas behind it. Launch speeds of 50–100 m/s are typical; we’ll use 75 m/s. (Nobody is allowed to build one, officially.)

Drag in two directions needs one new idea. How hard the air pushes depends on how fast the potato is moving through the air, which is its total speed. Which way it pushes is straight backward along the path. Split that one push into a sideways piece and an upward piece and you get

\[ a_x = -\frac{k}{m}\,v_x\,|V|, \qquad a_y = -g - \frac{k}{m}\,v_y\,|V|, \qquad |V| = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2} \]

The tempting shortcut is \(v_x|v_x|\) in the first formula and \(v_y|v_y|\) in the second, but that is not correct!

Here’s Tuesday’s two-direction throw with no drag, so everyone starts from the same place:

Our potato is a 5 cm × 8 cm slug, about 0.17 kg, with \(k/m = 0.00289\).

  1. Add drag, then turn it off. Put the two formulas into the loop. Then run it with k_over_m = 0 at 40° and compare to the no-drag range formula, \(R = v_0^2 \sin(2\theta)/g\).

  2. Estimate before you run. Right at launch, how big is the drag acceleration, \(\frac{k}{m} v_0^2\), compared to \(g\)? Based on Round 2, should drag matter a little or a lot?

  3. See the trajectory. Run the potato at 75 m/s and 40°, with and without drag, and plot both paths on the same axes: y against x, keeping only the points above ground. Add plt.gca().set_aspect("equal") so that one meter up looks the same as one meter across.

  4. Describe the difference. How much shorter is it? The no-drag path is symmetric: is the drag path? Where is its highest point, and how steeply does it come down compared to how it went up?

  5. Compare the landing speed to the launch speed, with and without drag. The no-drag potato lands at exactly its launch speed. Why does it have to? Then use what you found in Round 2 to work out the potato’s terminal velocity. Our launch speed is above it. What does that say about the first second of the flight?

Tuesday’s Challenge found that 45° throws farthest, when there’s no air. Drag just took about half our range at 40°. Is 45° still the best angle? Don’t answer that at your table. That’s HW 3.
