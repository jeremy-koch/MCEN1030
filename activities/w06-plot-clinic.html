Week 6 Thursday · 2 engagement credits

Activity for Thursday’s class. (Not a reading.)

A matplotlib cheat sheet

The full set of official cheat sheets lives at matplotlib.org/cheatsheets. They’re dense, but they’re great for “I know there’s a way to do this, what’s it called?”

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))                 # width, height (inches); call this before plotting

# drawing the data
plt.plot(x, y, "o")                        # markers only: "o" dots, "s" squares, "^" triangles
plt.plot(x, y, "o-")                       # markers joined by lines
plt.plot(x, y, "o", color="firebrick", markersize=8, linewidth=3, label="Route A")
plt.bar(names, heights)                    # bar chart; plt.barh(...) for sideways bars
plt.scatter(x, y)                          # another way to get dots

# words
plt.xlabel("Time (min)")                   # always with units!
plt.ylabel("Temperature (°C)")
plt.title("Coffee cools faster in ceramic")
plt.legend()                               # shows each plot's label=...
plt.text(20, 65, "some words")             # words at the point (x=20, y=65)
plt.annotate("windows opened", xy=(50, 1250), xytext=(55, 1150),
             arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->"))   # words with an arrow pointing at xy

# the axes
plt.xlim(0, 40)                            # which part of the x-axis shows
plt.ylim(0, 100)                           # which part of the y-axis shows
plt.yscale("log")                          # logarithmic y-axis
plt.xticks(rotation=45)                    # tilt the x labels so long names fit
plt.grid(True)

# reference lines and shading
plt.axhline(1000, color="gray", linestyle="--")   # horizontal line at y = 1000
plt.axvline(50, color="gray", linestyle=":")      # vertical line at x = 50
plt.axhspan(20, 30, alpha=0.2)                    # shaded band from y = 20 to y = 30

# a second y-axis on the right side (things plotted after this use it)
ax2 = plt.gca().twinx()

plt.tight_layout()                         # stop labels from overlapping
plt.show()

We’ll also want a few NumPy moves for reshaping the data before it gets plotted. For example, y[x >= 2000] keeps only the values where the matching x is at least 2000; np.mean(y) averages; np.cumsum(y) gives a running total; and np.argsort(y) gives the order that would sort y. Doing arithmetic on whole arrays (like 100 * (y - y[0]) / y[0] for percent change since the first value) is fair game too.

AI is welcome today for the syntax: “how do I put an arrow on a matplotlib plot?” is a perfectly good question to ask it. The choices (what to zoom, what to label, what story to tell) are the part we’re practicing, so those are yours.

An example we made earlier

Before you get your own dataset, let’s do one together, all the way through. We’ll look at a house in Boulder with solar panels on the roof, on one sunny October day. Every hour we have two numbers: how much power the panels were making, and how much the house was using, both in kilowatts (kW) and both averaged over that hour. Here’s the boring plot:

OK… so what’s the story? The orange hump is the sun and the other curve is the people. Look closer and we can see the panels make way more than the house needs around noon, but the house’s big moment is around 6–7 pm (people get home, start cooking, turn on lights), right as the sun is going down. The panels and the house are out of sync.

That’s the finding we want someone to walk away with, so let’s build the plot around it. This one pulls out a lot of tricks. None of them is required, but they’re all good to know exist:

The kWh numbers come from .sum(). Each hourly reading is a power (kW) held for one hour, so each one is a rectangle of energy (kW × 1 h = kWh), and adding up the rectangles gives the total. That’s area-as-a-sum-of-rectangles, doing actual work.

A tour of what’s going on in there:

  • fill_between colors the space between the two curves, with where= choosing which stretches get which color. The shaded area is energy, so the reader can literally see that there’s a lot more gold than purple.
  • axvspan shades nighttime gray, so we don’t need to explain why the solar is zero from 7 pm to 7 am. zorder=0 puts it behind everything else.
  • Direct labels (plt.text) sit right next to each curve, so the reader never has to glance back and forth at a legend.
  • annotate points an arrow at the one data point we want people to notice.
  • Custom ticks say “noon” and “6 pm” instead of 12 and 18. Small thing, but nobody has to translate.
  • The title states the finding instead of the topic. “Solar output and house use” makes the reader do the work. “The panels make plenty, just not when the house wants it” tells them what they’re looking at, and then the plot backs it up.
  • No top and right box lines (spines). This is pure taste, but it makes the plot feel less cluttered.

Is it honest? A quick check: the y-axis starts at zero, every number shown is a real reading, and a skeptic looking at the boring plot would agree with the title. I think it passes.

Now the evil twin. Same data, and it looks at least as responsible:

Every number here is true. The y-axis starts at zero, there are units, and the bars are labeled. Over the whole day, the panels really did make 46% more energy than the house used. But “solar-powered” suggests the house runs on sunshine, and the hourly plot says that’s not really true: the house bought 16.3 kWh from the grid, which is about 60% of everything it used, all of it after dark or before sunrise. Adding up the whole day threw away the one dimension where the story lives… time. (This is not a hypothetical trick, by the way. “Net” claims like this are everywhere. They aren’t wrong, exactly. They’re just answering a different question than the one you think you’re reading.)

If we were the honest table, our sentence might be: “Hourly averages from one sunny day. Shading shows when the house sent power to the grid vs. bought it.” If we were the misleading table: “Daily totals hide that the house buys ~60% of its electricity at night. The hourly version shows it.”

The plan

We have about 40 minutes of making, and then the reveal.

1. Open your dataset (down below; your CA will tell you which number). Read the description first. What was measured, in what units, and how many points are there? Run the cell. Out comes the boring plot: markers, labels, units, and nothing else. This is our control, and we’ll leave it alone. It’s the plot a skeptic would make if they had nothing to prove.

2. Look at the boring plot and talk at your table. What is the story here? What would a reader take away if all they saw was this? What’s the most interesting true thing in the data, and what’s the most tempting false thing? Both tables — honest and misleading — need to answer both questions. You can’t hide the truth well if you don’t know what it is.

3. Make the interesting plot. Now open your slip.

  • Honest tables: make the true takeaway jump off the page. Honest doesn’t mean timid. Zoom in if zooming shows something real, add a reference line if a comparison matters, annotate the moment something changed, and write a title that states the finding. Every choice you make should survive a skeptic who has seen the boring plot.
  • Misleading tables: make the room believe something false. The rules are that every number you plot must be real (straight from the dataset, or honestly computed from it: an average, a total, a percent). No typing in fake values. But you get to choose which numbers to show, which scale to use, what to compare, and what the title claims. Be subtle. The good misleading plots are the ones that look responsible.

4. Write one sentence on paper, and keep it face-down until the reveal:

  • Honest tables: the disclosure you’d put under the plot in a real report. For example, “The y-axis starts at 22 °C, room temperature, because that’s where both mugs end up.”
  • Misleading tables: the trick, and what an honest version would show. For example, “We only plotted March through August, which hides that this happens every year.”

5. Put both plots in the class slide deck, on your dataset’s two slides: the interesting plot on the first, the boring plot on the second. In the browser you can right-click (or long-press on an iPad) the plot and copy the image. A screenshot works too.

TipStuck?

Here are some questions that help from either side. What would change if the y-axis started at zero? What if we only saw half of the x-range? Is there a number that everything should be compared to: a threshold, a prediction, room temperature, last year? Would percent change tell a different story than raw numbers? Is anything in this dataset tiny compared to everything else?

The reveal

For each dataset, we’ll put the interesting plot on the screen for about ten seconds. You vote: thumbs up if you believe it, thumbs down if you think you’re being played. Then we flip to the boring plot, and the table reads its sentence.

Keep score in your head if you like. The misleading plots that fooled the most people and the honest plots that the most people trusted are both winners. More interesting, though, are the misses: an honest plot that the room didn’t trust, or a misleading one that everybody bought. What was it about that plot?

Deliverable (2 EC)

Your table’s two plots (boring and interesting) plus your one sentence. Everyone at the table submits the same thing.

Datasets

All fifteen datasets are realistic but made up for today. Each cell below is ready to run here in the browser, or you can copy it into Colab.

NoteDataset 1 — Boulder’s yearly average temperature

The average air temperature in Boulder for each year from 1950 to 2024, in °F. Each number is an average over all 365 days, so a single hot week barely moves it.

NoteDataset 2 — A laptop battery getting older

One laptop’s battery health, checked every 3 months from new: how much charge the battery can hold, as a percent of what it held on day one. Most manufacturers consider a battery “worn out” and worth replacing below 80%.

NoteDataset 3 — Two coffee mugs

Coffee poured at 85 °C into a regular ceramic mug and into a double-walled steel travel mug (lid off, same room), with a thermometer reading every 2 minutes for 40 minutes. The room is 22 °C.

NoteDataset 4 — Two bike routes to campus

One student biked to campus by two different routes and timed each trip, for 20 school days per route. Route A is the road; Route B is the creek path. Times are in minutes.

NoteDataset 5 — Where a home’s electricity goes

A year of electricity use in one all-electric Colorado home (it has a heat pump instead of a gas furnace), split up by what used it, in kilowatt-hours per year. The categories are listed alphabetically, which is how the utility’s report listed them.

NoteDataset 6 — Speed bumps on a neighborhood street

A residential street with a 25 mph speed limit. A radar sign logged every car, and these are the average speeds for each week, 10 weeks before and 10 weeks after the city installed speed bumps. The bumps went in during week 0 (no data that week). Neighbors are split on whether the bumps were worth it.

NoteDataset 7 — 3D-printed bracket strength

A small bracket, 3D-printed at different infill percentages (how solid the inside is: 10% is mostly air, 100% is solid plastic). Three brackets were printed at each setting and pulled until they broke. We have the breaking force, in newtons, and the print time for each, in minutes.

NoteDataset 8 — Fuel economy vs. speed

A midsize gas car driven at steady speeds on a flat test track, with fuel economy measured at each speed, in miles per gallon. Higher mpg means less fuel for the same trip.

NoteDataset 9 — E-scooter rides

Rides taken on a city’s shared e-scooter program each month, January 2023 through December 2024. The operator is trying to convince the city council to renew its contract.

NoteDataset 10 — Air in a full classroom

Carbon dioxide level in a packed 75-minute class, measured every 5 minutes, in parts per million (ppm). Outdoor air is about 420 ppm, and many building guidelines aim to keep rooms under about 1000 ppm, above which people start to feel drowsy and unfocused. Someone opened the windows partway through.

NoteDataset 11 — Snowfall at a ski area

Total snowfall for each ski season at one Colorado ski area, from the 1985 season to the 2024 season, in inches. (The ‘1985 season’ is the winter that started in fall 1985.)

NoteDataset 12 — Heat pump in the cold

A heat pump heats a house by moving heat in from outside air, even cold air. Its COP (coefficient of performance) is the units of heat delivered per unit of electricity used: COP = 3 means 3 kWh of heat for every 1 kWh from the wall. An ordinary electric space heater has a COP of exactly 1. These are one cold-climate heat pump’s COPs at different outdoor temperatures, in °F.

NoteDataset 13 — Chargers and burritos

Two things measured each year in one Colorado county, 2012 to 2024: the number of public EV charging ports, and the average price of a burrito at local restaurants, in dollars.

NoteDataset 14 — Rooftop solar in two towns

New rooftop solar systems installed each year in two Colorado towns, 2019 to 2024. Town A has about 98,000 people. Town B has about 1,200.

NoteDataset 15 — Stopping a car

How far a car travels between the moment a hazard appears and the moment it’s stopped, measured on a dry test track at different speeds, in feet. It comes in two parts: the reaction distance (the car keeps going while the driver notices and moves their foot to the brake) and the braking distance (brakes on, slowing to a stop). For scale, a car is about 15 ft long.
