Lights out

Week 10 Thursday · 2 engagement credits

On a windy night in January your power company shuts off electricity to your neighborhood. (This is happening more often on high-wind days due to fire risk.) It’s 20 °C inside and −10 °C outside, and no power means no furnace. Your landlord texts the only engineer they know, you: how long before we have to worry about the pipes?

(What is your guess for how long until the inside reaches 10 °C? Two hours? Ten? Two days?)

Part 1 — The model

In heat transfer you will learn that the heat transfer rate depends on the difference in temperature between two objects — when it is warm inside and cold outside, the house cools off faster, but that reduces the difference in temperature inside-outside, and so the cooling slows down with time. Mathematically there is one important number in the model: the object’s time constant, tau (τ, in hours). Each hour, the house loses about 1/τ of the gap. A drafty house has a small τ; a well-insulated one has a big τ. We’ll start with τ = 30 hours, which is a reasonable guess.

A house’s τ depends on its insulation, its windows, air sneaking under doors, how much heavy stuff is soaking up heat, etc. Somewhere between τ = 15 and 60 hours covers most houses.

Here’s the model written in Python. We’ve been writing Python for ten weeks, so you can read it already. Your job is to translate it into MATLAB, using the cheat sheet from this week.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

T_out = -10      # outside temperature (C), held constant
tau = 30         # house time constant (hours)
dt = 0.5         # time step (hours)
n = 96           # number of steps

t = np.arange(n + 1) * dt
T = np.zeros(n + 1)
T[0] = 20        # inside temperature when the power goes out (C)

for k in range(n):
    T[k+1] = T[k] - (T[k] - T_out) / tau * dt

plt.plot(t, T)
plt.xlabel("hours since the power went out")
plt.ylabel("inside temperature (C)")
plt.show()

  1. Translate it. The places to be careful: np.arange(n + 1), np.zeros(n + 1), T[0] = 20, and for k in range(n) together with T[k+1] and T[k]. Don’t use AI, get some experience looking at MATLAB!

  2. Check yourself, milestone by milestone. Type each of these in the Command Window as you go:

    check what you should see
    length(T) 97
    T(1) 20
    T(2) 19.5000
    T(3) 19.0083
    T(19) 12.1684
    T(end) -4.0242

    If one of them is off, you now know which part of the translation to look at: the first two catch a wrong start, problems with T(2) and T(3) point you to the loop update, and T(end) makes sure you have the right number of elements. (With one input, zeros makes a square matrix, not a list: length(T) still says 97, and the plot will look strange.)

  3. Look at the shape. Does it make sense? What value is it heading towards if we have many days where it stays at −10 °C? Does that make sense?

  4. Answer the landlord. This line finds the first time the inside is below 10 °C. You should get 12.5.

    t(find(T < 10, 1))

    find(T < 10, 1) gives the index of the first element of T that’s below 10, and then t(...) looks up the time at that index. How does the answer compare with your guess?

LLMs are very good at translating code between languages, but the point of this exercise is to give you some experience looking through MATLAB code.

Part 2 — Three ways to be wrong

You’ll get a nice plot, but that does not necessarily mean you should believe the result! Engineers sort the ways a model can mislead into three kinds:

  • Numerical. The model is fine, but the way we computed it isn’t. Chopping time into steps that are too big is the classic one; we saw it with the balloon in week 5.
  • Physical. The model leaves out something that matters for this situation: a missing effect.
  • Extrapolation. The model was fine where it was tested, but we use it in conditions far from where it was tested.

Below are three situations. For each, your group writes down which kind it is, the evidence that tells you, and what check would have caught it.

Situation A: “Faster”

Your roommate thinks the simulation has too many steps. They change dt to 10 hours and n to 6, so it still covers about the same stretch of time.

Try it. Then try dt = 20, 30, 45, 60, and 70 (keep n = 6). Look at the plots, and look at the actual numbers in T.

  • At what dt does the answer stop being a bit off and start being ridiculous? What is ridiculous about it? (Think about what the outside temperature is.)
  • At dt = 0.5 and dt = 0.25, when does the house reach 10 °C? Does halving the step change the answer much? What does that tell you about whether 0.5 hours is small enough?

Situation B: “It was a sunny day”

A neighbor had their thermostat app logging during an outage last winter, with power out for a full day starting at 6 p.m. Outside stayed around −5 °C the whole time. (These are made-up numbers, but they’re realistic.)

time 6 pm 8 pm 10 pm 12 am 2 am 4 am 6 am 8 am 10 am 12 pm 2 pm 4 pm 6 pm
inside (°C) 20.0 18.4 16.9 15.5 14.1 12.9 11.8 10.7 10.0 11.4 13.2 13.4 12.2

Change your model to T_out = -5 and dt = 0.5, run it for 24 hours (what’s n?), and compare with the log. Typing these two lines plots the log on top of your model:

hold on
plot(0:2:24, [20.0 18.4 16.9 15.5 14.1 12.9 11.8 10.7 10.0 11.4 13.2 13.4 12.2], 'o')
  • Where do the model and the log agree, and where do they split?
  • The house got warmer with no furnace and a −5 °C day. What could do that? Can our model ever do that, no matter what tau we pick?
  • Is the model useless now? For the landlord’s question about the first night, should we trust it?

Situation C: “Just tell them it’s fine”

Suppose a property manager tested this exact model against five real outages last winter. Each outage lasted less than 12 hours, on nights between −5 and 5 °C, and the model matched the thermostat logs within about 1 °C every time. Now they want to tell tenants: “our model shows the pipes will be fine through a three-day outage at −25 °C.”

  • What is different in the present situation vs. the ones the property manager looked at?
  • Physically, why might the pipes be damaged earlier than predicted?

Part 3 — A what-if (if you have time)

Back to T_out = -10, dt = 0.5, and enough steps for 100 hours. Put hold on after the plot line, then run the model three times: τ = 15, 30, and 60 hours. For each, determine when the house reaches 10 °C.

What happens to that time when τ doubles? Is that what you’d have guessed?

Deliverable (2 EC)

Your group’s sheet, names at the top:

  1. Everyone’s guess, and the model’s answer for 10 °C.
  2. The values of T(end), etc., confirming they match with Python’s results.
  3. For each of Situations A, B, and C: what is wrong and why?
  4. If you got to Part 3: the three times, and one sentence on the pattern.
