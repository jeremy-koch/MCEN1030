Wind farm design

Week 3 Thursday · 2 engagement credits

Standards seeded: P1, A1, A4 · You’ll need: a laptop with Colab open, and a phone or paper for the hand-checks.

Today we build three functions that hand results to one another, and then spend some time deciding whether to trust what they say.

The problem

Your firm is scoping a wind turbine for the eastern plains outside Denver. You need a tool that answers: for a given rotor diameter and a given wind speed, how much power comes out?

The swept area. The blades sweep a circle, and only the wind passing through that circle is available to you. From the rotor diameter \(D\):

\[ A = \frac{\pi D^2}{4} \]

The power in the wind, and the share you capture. Moving air has kinetic energy, and the faster it moves the more it carries — dramatically more, because velocity shows up cubed:

\[ P = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho\,A\,v^3\,C_p \]

where \(\rho\) is air density and \(C_p\) is the fraction of the wind’s energy the turbine actually captures. Take \(\rho = 1.0\) kg/m³ for Denver’s thin air as the default value.

The efficiency, which is not a constant. Blades are shaped to capture the wind best at one particular speed — too slow and there is no aerodynamic lift; too fast and they stall and spill. So \(C_p\) peaks at a design speed and falls off on either side:

\[ C_p(v) = C_{p,\text{max}}\left[1 - \left(\frac{v - v_{\text{best}}}{w}\right)^2\right] \]

with \(C_{p,\text{max}} = 0.45\), \(v_{\text{best}} = 11\) m/s, and \(w = 8\) m/s.

Round 0

An LLM was asked for the power function and handed back a number: for a 100 m rotor in a 10 m/s wind, 52 kilowatts.

Do you believe it? Discuss with your group

Round 1 — three specs

Everyone create a new Colab. Make your first cell a “text” cell, and write specifications for the three functions. Decide what the required inputs, optional inputs, and outputs are of each function, and pay attention to the units. You may not agree with your neighbor, but you may both be reasonable.

Make your second cell a text cell as well, and do a hand-check of the \(D = 100\) m case.

The area function you check with a calculator.

The efficiency function you can check against a physical bound. No turbine can capture more than about 59% of the wind’s energy. Any \(C_p\) your function returns above 0.59 is wrong. You can calculate it too: what should it give at \(v = 11\) m/s?

The power function you check against common sense after plugging-in reasonable values. Real 100 m turbines produce 2–3 MW… it’s understandable if you didn’t know that number today (I didn’t until I wrote this), but with time/experience in engineering you’ll start to have intuition about numbers. Clearly 52 kW is low, and should set off alarm bells!

Round 2 — write and audit the functions

Now prompt for the code (or write it from scratch… good practice). One function at a time or all three at once — your call, and maybe your group tries both ways and you talk about how it went.

Then annotate what comes back: for each function, write one line in your own words describing what it does. Include this as a docstring.

Three functions?

My intention is for you to create three functions. The area one is pretty simple, but the other two contain separate physics and are worth keeping separate. The efficiency function varies between blade designs, and so having that as its own function that is called by the power function is better practice.

So: do you have three functions, or did you/the LLM combine them in some way? Add a comment in your code about this.

Round 3 — testing

Run your hand-check case. If you get agreement, that is a good step towards verification. If not, track down the error — was it you, or the code?

Once your chain is verified, run it twice more: \(v = 11\) m/s, then \(v = 15\) m/s.

At 15 m/s the turbine is much less efficient — about 0.34 against the peak 0.45… but you should see it makes more power. There’s more energy in a faster wind, and it is worth wasting energy to extract more of it.

So the most efficient operating point isn’t the most productive one. Where exactly is the best wind speed? Run your code a few times to try to find it, but it is tedious… we’ll talk about strategies for optimizing this soon!

Deliverable — 2 EC, per person

Submitted individually this week, not per table. Export a pdf from Colab

  • Your three specs, as written before you prompted.
  • The prompt you used, plus the model and roughly when you ran it.
  • Your annotated code.
  • Your hand-check and discussion.
  • Discussion of if there were three functions.
  • One sentence (add as a text cell at the bottom): something interesting that someone else included in their spec that you left out.
